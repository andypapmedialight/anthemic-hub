Technics and Time

What does a tool, a machine, or a digital memory prosthesis do to the subject who uses it — and who is using whom? This fourth branch leaves Freudo-Marxism and Western Marxism's culture maps where they are, and follows a different quarrel: Aristotle's techne, Heidegger's Gestell, Simondon's technical individuation, Stiegler's pharmacology, Yuk Hui's cosmotechnics, and Wendy Chun's software-as-memory.

NOTE — Genealogies of Desire keeps Heidegger at Being and Time (1927), folded into Kojève's recognition seminar, and deliberately does not follow the later work or the political catastrophe of his Nazism. This is that deferred genealogy: the question of technology, not Dasein as care. Same caveats as the siblings — intellectual history, not engineering; "how influential" and "what it was reacting against," not "how well-evidenced." McLuhan, Land, Plant, and Haraway appear only as cross-strains: parallel tech diagnoses, not this lineage's trunk. Merchant, Hayles, and Chun are the feminist/STS foils on that same parallel band — not a fifth Heidegger school.

Aristotle's techne c. 350 BCE, making vs knowing how to live

technephronesispoiesis

The vocabulary the Gestell argument keeps returning to starts in ordinary Greek philosophy of craft. Aristotle, in the Nicomachean Ethics and related texts, distinguishes techne — skilled making, the know-how that produces an artifact according to a rule — from phronesis, practical wisdom about how to live well among people, and from theoretical knowledge of what is necessarily so. A shoemaker has techne; a statesman needs phronesis. Poiesis is the bringing-into-being that techne serves: the house is for dwelling, the shoe for walking, and the maker's excellence is judged by the work, not by the maker's character as a citizen.

That split matters here because almost every later figure in the line is arguing, under different names, about whether modern technology still fits Aristotle's picture — a subordinate craft serving human ends — or whether the relation has inverted, so that ends themselves are reorganized by technical systems. The Grounding timeline already pins Plato·Aristotle for akrasia and the divided psyche; the techne line only needs the craft vocabulary. Heidegger will claim modern Technik is not techne at all in Aristotle's sense. Stiegler will claim human memory was always already technical. Hui will ask whether Aristotle's Greek cut between techne and cosmology is itself a provincial starting point.

how this landed

Techne — ordinary Greek for craft and art; later English "technique" and "technology" inherit the word while losing Aristotle's subordination of making to living well.

Not a full Ethics — this pin is lexical and contrastive, not a reading of Aristotle's politics or metaphysics.

Heidegger's Gestell 1953–54, the question concerning technology

Gestellstanding-reserveenframingrevealing

The Heidegger who matters for Gestell is not the 1927 author of Being and Time that Genealogies folds into Kojève, but the postwar lecturer of "The Question Concerning Technology" (delivered 1953; published 1954). There Martin Heidegger argues that the essence of modern technology is not any particular machine, and not even instrumental means-ends thinking as usually understood. It is Gestell — usually translated enframing — a mode of revealing that challenges nature forth as standing-reserve (Bestand): everything appears as stock on call for ordering, optimizing, and further ordering. A river becomes hydroelectric potential; a forest becomes lumber inventory; a human being becomes human resource. Against the comforting story that technology is a neutral tool we can simply use well or badly, Heidegger claims Gestell already decides in advance what counts as real.

He still hears an older echo in the word: Greek techne was a way of bringing-forth (poiesis), closer to art than to industrial challenging-forth. Modern Technik, on his reading, is a extreme of that revealing that has forgotten its own character as revealing. The political catastrophe of his Nazism — party membership, the rectorate, the postwar silences — is not a side note and not this essay's subject: it is why many readers refuse to treat his technology diagnosis as detachable wisdom. Stiegler, Simondon's readers, and Hui all take Gestell as a load-bearing foil or ancestor — which is why the technology argument stays live, not because the biography is settled or forgiven.

Fundamental ontology after the "turn," the full Hölderlin and poetry lectures, and a rehabilitation brief are another history. The sibling desire genealogy already said those are their own maps. The bridge from 1927 Dasein to 1954 Gestell is real — care and worldhood make the later claim about revealing intelligible — but the quarrel changes: from how a finite existence is thrown into a world of tools, to how a planetary technical revealing reorganizes worldhood itself.

how this landed

Gestell — still the default continental reference for "technology as world-disclosure, not just tools"; contested for vagueness and for underplaying political economy.

Nazism — documented and non-negotiable; readers who use the technology essay anyway usually do so under protest or with heavy caveat, as here.

Being and Time — stays on the desire genealogy; do not collapse the two Heideggers into one box.

Simondon 1958, technical objects and individuation

individuationtechnical objectconcretizationassociated milieu

Gilbert Simondon's doctoral work — On the Mode of Existence of Technical Objects (1958) and the larger individuation thesis — is the main philosophical precursor Stiegler will credit. Against both the hylomorphic picture (form stamped on passive matter) and a romantic humanism that treats machines as alienated spirit, Simondon asks how technical objects themselves individuate: how a crude abstract machine becomes a more concrete one as its elements take on multifunctional roles and couple to an associated milieu. A technical being is not a dead tool waiting for a human essence to animate it; it has a genesis, a metastability, and a relation to the world that philosophy of technology had mostly ignored.

That move matters because it refuses Heidegger's tendency to treat Technik as a monolithic revealing with no internal history of machines. Simondon still diagnoses alienation — culture's contempt for technics, the split between specialist and user — but the therapy is different: culture must learn to think with technical individuation rather than only fear Gestell. Deleuze later picks up Simondon's individuation vocabulary; Stiegler will rebuild it into an account of human memory as always already technical.

how this landed

Simondon — long marginal in Anglophone theory; stature rose sharply via Deleuze studies and then Stiegler; still denser and less "quotable" than Heidegger's technology essay.

Not cybernetics lite — individuation is a general ontology of becoming, of which technical objects are one privileged case, not a gadget theory.

Stiegler 1994–2020, Technics and Time

tertiary retentionpharmacologyorganologyepiphilogenesis

Bernard Stiegler's Technics and Time trilogy (1994–2001) is the spine and namesake of the argument. His wager: the human is not a biological given that later invents tools; technical exteriorization is constitutive of who "we" are. Against a clean inside/outside of mind and machine, he reads paleoanthropology (especially Leroi-Gourhan) as showing that tools and exteriorized memory — flint, writing, archives, hard drives — are how a mortal, incomplete being inherits a past it did not live. He calls that exteriorized memory tertiary retention, extending Husserl's primary retention (the just-past in a melody) and secondary retention (reactivated memory): the third is the technical trace that outlives any living consciousness.

From there, technics is always a pharmakon in the double sense Derrida recovered from Plato's pharmacy: remedy and poison at once. Writing supports memory and threatens living recollection; industrial temporal objects (film, television, later platforms) can open attention or capture and proletarianize it. Stiegler's organology treats psychic, social, and technical organs as co-evolving systems — an account that can absorb Heidegger's warning about Gestell without accepting technology as a single destiny, and that can absorb Simondon's genesis of technical objects without leaving politics and desire outside. Late work turns harder toward disruption, attention economy, and the need for a new critique of political economy of the digital; not every pamphlet is followed, but the pharmacological and organological frame is the lasting export.

An Anglophone rhyme, not a student: Andy Clark and David Chalmers's “The Extended Mind” (1998) argues cognition already leaks into notebooks, phones, and other prostheses — the mind is not skull-bound. That is not Stiegler's tertiary retention (which is about how a mortal inherits a past it did not live), but it is the same refusal of a clean inside/outside — extended mind as coupling in the present. N. Katherine Hayles, in the cross-strains below, is the needed brake: information-without-bodies was cybernetics' dream; extended mind can sound like that dream unless embodiment stays in the account. Clark also writes in the predictive-processing idiom now plotted on the consciousness map; the pin here is only the leak into tools.

how this landed

Technics and Time — still the entry point; dense, and often taught selectively (tertiary retention + pharmakon) rather than cover to cover.

Pharmakon — via Derrida's Plato reading; Stiegler politicizes it for industrial and digital technics.

Clark / extended mind — analytic-philosophy standard; the 1998 paper is co-authored with Chalmers. Parallel to tertiary retention, not a French inheritance. Hayles is the embodiment foil so the leak into tools does not become information-without-bodies.

Biography — Stiegler's early imprisonment and self-education are part of his public mythos; useful context, not a substitute for the arguments.

Yuk Hui 2016–, cosmotechnics

cosmotechnicsplural technicsafter Gestell

Yuk Hui's The Question Concerning Technology in China (2016) takes Heidegger's title and refuses his European frame as universal. If Gestell names a particular modern Western revealing, then treating it as the essence of technology repeats the provincialism it diagnoses. Hui's counter-concept is cosmotechnics: the unification, in a given culture, of cosmic and moral order with technical activity — not one Technik, but historically plural ways technical practice was bound to cosmology. Chinese thought, on his reading, offers resources for thinking technics otherwise than as standing-reserve, without nostalgia for a pre-technical Eden and without simply importing Heidegger as Asia's destiny.

Later essays and Recursivity and Contingency push the same pluralizing pressure against a homogenized digital globalism — not an Axial cameo, a contemporary argument that the Gestell story is one history among others. Stiegler remains a major interlocutor; the disagreement is whether organology still needs a stronger dose of cultural and cosmological difference than European philosophy of technology usually allows.

how this landed

Cosmotechnics — widely cited in media theory, art, and STS-adjacent philosophy; still contested by sinologists over how cleanly "Chinese technics" can be periodized against Heidegger.

Not anti-tech — Hui's project is reinvention and pluralism, not Luddism or civilizational essentialism.

Cross-strains parallels, not this trunk

McLuhanaccelerationismcyberfeminismsoftware as memory

Marshall McLuhan, covered in Constellations of History, diagnoses media as environments that remake the sensorium — "the medium is the message" — without Heidegger's Gestell vocabulary or Stiegler's tertiary retention. Useful rhyme: both treat technical form as world-shaping. Different quarrel: McLuhan wants the environment made visible; Cultural Studies then rejects his determinism. That fight is Cultural Studies' own, not restaged as a Gestell dispute.

Nick Land and Warwick accelerationism treat capital/technics as an autonomous optimizing process to be pushed further; Noys names and refuses that program. Stiegler's pharmacology can sound adjacent — technics as fate-shaped — but his politics run toward care, education, and reinvention of the pharmakon, not Meltdown. Keep them parallel.

N. Katherine Hayles's How We Became Posthuman (1999) is the feminist STS foil after Stiegler: against cybernetics' dream of information without bodies, she insists on embodiment and materiality — a parallel to Haraway's cyborg and a useful foil to any Gestell story that forgets gender and labor. She is also the brake on Clark's extended mind above: leaking cognition into tools is not the same as deleting the body. She is not Stiegler's school; she is why the techne line cannot stay only Heidegger–Simondon–Stiegler–Hui.

Carolyn Merchant's The Death of Nature (1980) is the ecofeminist precursor: the scientific revolution, she argues, remade nature as dead, controllable matter — a gendered domination story that rhymes with Gestell without sharing Heidegger's vocabulary. Merchant sits beside, not inside, the Simondon–Stiegler trunk: another feminist pressure that techne is never neutral craft.

Wendy Hui Kyong Chun's Programmed Visions (2011) is the software-studies foil after Hayles: software is not a transparent tool but a memory that regenerates the past as if it were the present — newness as ideology. Code that appears to execute the future is also how race, sex, and class get stored and replayed. Discriminating Data (2021) is the later pin, not a second box: homophily and machine learning as the technical form of segregation. She sits between Hayles's embodiment brake and Hui's cosmotechnics: an STS argument that tertiary retention is never a neutral archive. Not Stiegler's school; the reason digital memory cannot stay only Husserl-plus-hard-drive.

Donna Haraway's cyborg and Sadie Plant's cyberfeminism (and Xenofeminism downstream) are feminist technics that refuse both pure nature and pure Gestell-despair. They belong on the desire genealogy's map; they appear here only so the timeline can show the kinship without pretending Stiegler and Haraway share a school.

how this landed

Why list them — reviewers of these maps keep asking where techne meets acceleration and media theory; the answer is "beside, not inside," and this section is that answer in prose.

Hayles — still a default posthumanities reference; the Wellek Prize book is the pin, not her whole later corpus.

Merchant — foundational for ecofeminist history of science; contested by some historians of early modern science over how cleanly Baconian "rape of nature" metaphors drive the whole story.

Chun — Programmed Visions (2011) is the map pin; Discriminating Data (2021) is argued in the same paragraph, not as a second box. Software-studies / STS default; Korean-Canadian, writing in English.

The desire genealogy asked whose interests the unconscious serves. The history constellation asked what a cultural form does politically by existing. This branch asks a third question: what technics do to time, memory, and the possibility of a subject who could still care. Gestell says the revealing has already decided. Simondon and Stiegler say technical genesis and pharmacology leave the decision open — poisonous and curative at once. Hui says even that European frame is one cosmotechnics among others.

None of those answers settles Freudo-Marxism or Western Marxism. A hard drive can be tertiary retention and, completely independently, a node in communicative capitalism or standing-reserve logistics — or, on Chun's account, a programmed vision that stores race and class as if they were the future. The maps are meant to sit beside each other, not collapse into one master diagram.
