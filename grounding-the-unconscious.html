Grounding the Unconscious

Can the id, the ego-ideal, or Lacan's objet a be more than useful fictions — actual patterns in chemistry, circuitry, and the language a brain is soaked in during development? A survey of what maps, what doesn't, and who's actually done the work.

Prehistory of the unconscious ~1200 BCE–1900, dreams and drives before Freud

Freud didn't invent the idea that people are moved by forces they can't see or control — he inherited a very long argument about it, and that argument does not begin in classical Athens. Long before Greek philosophy named the psyche, Egyptian and Mesopotamian dream books and omen literature already treated dreams as meaningful, coded traffic with gods, kings, and futures — not noise — a craft of interpretation Freud later surveys as a precursor genre when he opens The Interpretation of Dreams. The point is not Egyptology: it is that "something in you speaks while you sleep, and someone has to decode it" is older than the Greek soul-models below.

Another early non-Greek dualism worth marking, even though later Iranian religion is another history: Zoroaster (Zarathustra; dating wildly contested, often pinned somewhere in the later second–first millennium BCE). The Avestan picture of a cosmos split between truth/order and the lie, with human choice caught between them, is not a Freudian unconscious — but it is already a claim that the decisive forces in a life are not exhausted by what a person consciously endorses. Nietzsche will later steal the name for Thus Spoke Zarathustra; that literary reuse is not a historical transmission of Zoroastrian psychology into Freud.

A parallel Axial-age stream, outside the Greek line entirely, already has desire and ignorance doing structural work. The early Upanishads (roughly 8th–5th centuries BCE) treat craving and misrecognition — avidya, not-knowing what the self actually is — as what binds a person to suffering, with waking life itself often read as a kind of dream relative to a deeper ground (atman/brahman). That is not Freud's dynamic unconscious, but it is already a picture in which the conscious agent is not the last word on what moves it. Schopenhauer, below, will read this tradition hard into his blind Will; the neuroscience sections later never close that debt.

Confucius (Kongzi; traditional dates 551–479 BCE) is thinner on dream-decoding and a hidden drive economy — his problem is ritual, virtue, and the cultivated self in a social order. What still belongs with the Axial peers is the insistence that self-knowledge and right action are achieved through long formation (xiushen), not by transparent introspection alone: you can mean well and still be wrongly aligned. Neo-Confucianism and modern East Asian philosophy are another history; the Axial claim is that the agent is not simply given to itself.

Laozi (conventional attribution of the Daodejing; authorship and date themselves contested) pushes further from the other Chinese side: the Dao that can be fully named is not the constant Dao, and wu wei — non-forcing action — treats effective agency as something that can miscarry when the deliberating self tries to grip too hard. Again, not repression; but a picture in which conscious will is not the master process. Keep Confucius and Laozi as distinct boxes: shared century-neighborhood, different diagnoses.

The Buddha (usually placed in the 5th century BCE) radicalizes the South Asian neighborhood already named above: craving (taṇhā) and dependent origination make desire and ignorance the engine of the cycle, not a private pathology bolted onto an otherwise rational self. Again, not repression-with-return — but a systematic claim that what you take yourself to want, and why, is not transparent from the inside. Abhidharma and modern mindfulness research are another history; the mark is the non-Greek peer that sits next to the Greek prehistory Plato inherits.

Inside the Greek world, the pre-Socratics already stage cosmic and psychic forces that reason does not simply author. Heraclitus makes logos and a deep psyche whose boundaries you will not find by walking — flux and measure beneath ordinary opinion. Parmenides answers from the other extreme: only Being is; the world of change and mortal opinion (doxa) is a path of seeming — a crack between what seems and what is that Plato will never stop answering. Empedocles pairs Love and Strife as world-moving powers that also organize living compounds — an early template for reading attraction and conflict as more than local moods. Democritus (with Leucippus) gives the rival materialist cut: atoms and void, a soul made of fine atoms, thought as a physical process — the ancestor-line that will later make Churchland-style replacement of folk psychology thinkable, long before anyone has that vocabulary. These are cosmologies first; the later Freudian temptation to hear Eros and the death drive in Empedocles, or a proto-eliminativism in Democritus, is retrospective. What matters is the refusal, already archaic, to treat the thinking subject as the sole mover of what happens in a life.

Socrates (d. 399 BCE) is the hinge into the moral psychology Plato writes down. The Delphic "know thyself," the elenchus that shows people cannot give a consistent account of the virtues they claim to live by, and the daimonion — an inner warning voice Socrates treats as other than his deliberating self — all press the same crack: you can be sincerely wrong about what moves you, and something in you can check or compel without being identical to conscious choice. Plato's dialogues make that Socratic practice into metaphysics; Aristotle will rework it as akrasia. The timeline keeps Socrates distinct from the Plato–Aristotle box because the problem of opaque motive is already his before the tripartite soul is drawn.

Plato's tripartite soul (The Republic) already splits the psyche into reason, a spirited/assertive part, and appetite, with appetite an unruly, non-rational force reason has to govern rather than fully understand — not a dynamic unconscious with repression, but an early version of the same problem: something in you wants things your reasoning self doesn't endorse. Akrasia — acting against your own better judgment, discussed at length by both Plato and Aristotle — is the same puzzle from another angle. And well before Freud made dreams his royal road, Artemidorus's Oneirocritica (2nd century CE) had already treated them as meaningful, coded messages requiring interpretation rather than noise — a genre Freud engages directly in the literature survey that opens The Interpretation of Dreams, continuous with the Near Eastern craft above even when the metaphysics have changed.

Between that classical inheritance and the Enlightenment accounts of unconscious desire sits the figure who sets the modern terms for "what a mind even is." René Descartes's Meditations on First Philosophy (1641) reaches cogito ergo sum by radical doubt: every external belief can be suspended, but the act of thinking cannot doubt itself away — so a thinking subject is certain even when the world is not. From that certainty he argues mind–body dualism: mind as res cogitans (thinking, non-extended substance) and body as res extensa (extended, mechanistic matter), really distinct kinds of thing. That split is the load-bearing background problem later names keep rediscovering — how a thinking "I" relates to a body, whether the self is a substance or a function, whether experience is something physics can close. Descartes himself is plotted as the non-computable, hard-problem corner of the consciousness map; what matters is his place in the prehistory of the subject the unconscious later complicates. The desire genealogy's Kant section is the next philosophical beat: the Paralogisms keep the "I think" as a formal unity of experience while refusing Descartes's leap to a soul-substance — subject as function, not thing. Spinoza and Leibniz, next, keep a thinking subject but dissolve or complicate the clean dualist cut.

The specifically philosophical unconscious starts taking modern shape in the generation after him. Spinoza's Ethics (1677) — a monist reply to Cartesian dualism, one substance under two attributes rather than two substances — argues people are conscious of their desires and ignorant of the causes that produce them — a mind that knows what it wants without knowing why is already most of the way to a Freudian claim. Leibniz went further in the New Essays on Human Understanding (written c. 1704; published only in 1765), proposing petites perceptions — countless minute perceptions below the threshold of awareness that nonetheless shape conscious experience (his example: any single wave's sound is imperceptible, but the surf's roar, made of thousands of them, isn't) — arguably the first clean statement that unconscious mental activity is doing real work all the time, not just in special pathological cases.

The 19th century, especially in German-speaking Europe, is where this becomes unavoidable background for Freud specifically. Johann Friedrich Herbart's dynamic psychology (1820s), part of the Austrian school curriculum Freud grew up in, modeled ideas as competing for a limited store of consciousness, with weaker ideas pushed below a threshold of awareness by stronger, opposing ones — a direct conceptual ancestor of repression, in the vocabulary of forces and thresholds rather than persons and memories. Arthur Schopenhauer's Will (The World as Will and Representation, 1819; issued late 1818) is the blind, non-rational striving underlying all behavior, with intellect a secondary, often self-deceived servant of drives it doesn't control — probably the single most-cited philosophical ancestor of the id. And Eduard von Hartmann's Philosophy of the Unconscious (1869) was a genuine publishing phenomenon, running through many editions and making das Unbewusste a fashionable, widely discussed term in educated German culture two decades before Freud's own clinical work began — whatever Freud discovered clinically, he didn't discover the word, or the idea that there was something worth calling by it.

Nietzsche is the case Freud himself handled most awkwardly. On the Genealogy of Morals (1887) already has an account of conscience forming when outward-directed instinct gets turned back against the self and internalized — read next to Freud's superego, the overlap is hard to miss — and Nietzsche's drives (Triebe), his picture of the self as a contested multiplicity rather than a unified subject, and his insistence that conscious reasoning mostly rationalizes motives fixed elsewhere in the body, all anticipate specific Freudian claims closely enough that Freud's own students and biographers noted it directly. Freud's own response, by most biographical accounts, was to avoid reading Nietzsche closely for years, reportedly out of a wish to reach his own conclusions before risking finding them already written — a rare case where the anxiety of influence is part of the documented record rather than a later critic's inference.

Closer to Freud's own bench, the influence is clinical rather than literary. Jean-Martin Charcot, whose Paris lectures on hysteria Freud attended in 1885, demonstrated hysterical symptoms could be produced and removed by hypnotic suggestion — direct evidence hysteria was psychologically, not just organically, caused, and the single most important push toward Freud's own turn from neurology to psychology. Hypnosis itself, descending from Mesmer's animal magnetism through the Nancy School's Hippolyte Bernheim (with whom Freud also studied), supplied the working method — inducing an altered state to access material unavailable to ordinary waking recall — that Freud used with Breuer before replacing it with free association. Pierre Janet, a direct contemporary, was developing an overlapping theory of dissociation and split-off "fixed ideas" in traumatized patients at almost exactly the same time as Freud and Breuer's own hysteria work, and later, not without some justice, accused Freud of insufficient credit. Gustav Fechner's psychophysics gave Freud an explicit model for treating mental activity as a quantifiable, conserved economy — Freud names Fechner directly as the source of the constancy principle underneath the pleasure principle.

William James is the American peer this German-clinical corridor usually skips. The Principles of Psychology (1890) already treats consciousness as a stream, not a Cartesian inner theatre, and his later radical empiricism makes “pure experience” prior to the subject/object split — a cousin of the monism Spinoza opened above, not a footnote to Freud. The “hidden self” and the subliminal (partly via F. W. H. Myers) are a rival pre-Freudian unconscious: threshold and fringe, not repression-with-return. James also floats a filter theory of the brain — the organ as reducing valve on a wider field — that Huxley later popularizes and that Kastrup still recruits. He and Freud shared a platform at Clark University in 1909; the timeline keeps him as a living contemporary, not as a German ancestor.

The standard scholarly account of all this is Henri Ellenberger's The Discovery of the Unconscious (1970), and its actual thesis is worth stating plainly: Freud didn't discover the unconscious so much as synthesize, systematize, and clinically operationalize an idea already thoroughly in the air of his intellectual world, then present the result as more of a solitary discovery than the record supports. That's a real complication to sit next to Crews and Grünbaum below — not a charge of fraud like Crews's, but a challenge to originality rather than validity, and one Freud's own handling of Nietzsche suggests he was, at some level, aware of himself.

how this landed

Near East / Zoroaster / Upanishads / Buddha / Confucius · Laozi — marked here as structural peers for opaque desire, dual cosmic forces, or a self that is not simply transparent to itself — not as "sources of Freud" in a citation sense. Schopenhauer's Upanishadic reading is the clearest documented bridge into the German line below. Zoroaster's dates, Laozi's authorship, early Upanishads, and the historical Buddha remain contested (often by a century or more); years on the Thinkers Timeline are conventional pins, not consensus birth–death dates. Confucius and Laozi are thinner for dream/drive specifically; they stay because Axial self-cultivation and Daoist non-mastery still crack the "transparent agent" assumption.

Socrates / pre-Socratics — load-bearing for the Greek moral-psychology corridor into Plato; not claimed as clinical ancestors of the id. Socrates is usually pinned to his death (399 BCE); Empedocles is often placed around the mid-440s BCE; Parmenides and Democritus frame the Being-vs-seeming and atomist alternatives Plato inherits or resists.

Descartes — still the default origin story for modern philosophy of mind; dualism is a minority position among working scientists, but the mind–body problem he named did not go away — Chalmers's hard problem and property dualism, below, are late descendants of the same gap. For the epistemological break with soul-substance, see Kant's Paralogisms on the desire page.

Ellenberger — his synthesis thesis is now the mainstream historical consensus; the exact weight of any single influence (Herbart vs. Schopenhauer vs. von Hartmann) on Freud personally remains genuinely debated among historians.

Nietzsche — the parallels are widely acknowledged; whether Freud read enough of him early enough to have been directly shaped, rather than independently converging on similar ideas, is still argued over.

Janet — most historians now credit both men with largely independent, overlapping discovery, rather than treating either as derivative of the other.

James — standard in American psychology's origin story, under-read in Freudian prehistory; the 1909 Clark meeting is documented, the filter theory is the live recruitment into later idealism, not a claim that Freud comes out of Harvard.

Herbart — his direct personal influence on Freud is inferred from shared vocabulary and curriculum, not from any citation by Freud himself.

Freud 1900–1939, Dreams to death

structural modeldrive theory

The topographical model (1900) splits mind into unconscious, preconscious, and conscious by accessibility. The later structural model (1923, The Ego and the Id) splits it by function instead: the id is the inherited reservoir of drives (Eros and the death drive), running on primary process — condensation, displacement, no contradiction, no time. The ego is what forms at the interface with the external world, doing reality-testing and delaying gratification. The superego is the internalized parental/social prohibition; its positive pole, aimed at rather than forbidding, is the ego-ideal — the image of who one ought to become.

That split runs on two different emotional economies. The prohibitive superego ("thou shalt not") is modeled on the feared parent and punishes transgression with guilt — backward-looking, reacting to what's already been done. The ego-ideal ("thou shalt") is modeled on the admired parent, or, in Freud's earlier essay "On Narcissism" (1914), on the infant's own lost sense of being perfect — "His Majesty the Baby" — later displaced onto an external image to grow into. It's forward-looking: falling short produces shame, approximating it produces pride, not guilt. Freud never fully settled whether these are one agency or two; Lacan later re-splits the terminology again, relocating Freud's narcissistic ideal-image to what he calls the ideal-ego, and reserving ego-ideal for something else entirely (below) — so the same two words point to different things in the two authors.

Freud's own throwaway line matters more than it's usually given credit for: "the ego is first and foremost a bodily ego." He meant the ego starts as a projection of the surface of the body, not an abstract reasoning module bolted onto meat — which turns out to be a genuinely useful hook for the neuroscience below.

A biographical footnote: cocaine

Before any of this, Freud was a young neurologist chasing a breakthrough, and for a few years in the 1880s he thought he'd found one in cocaine. His 1884 monograph Über Coca promoted the drug enthusiastically — for depression, for fatigue, and, disastrously, as a cure for morphine addiction. He used it himself, sent some to his fiancée Martha Bernays, and pushed it on colleagues. One of them, his close friend Ernst von Fleischl-Marxow, was already dependent on morphine for nerve pain; Freud's cocaine "cure" left him addicted to both at once, in a decline most accounts say hastened his death in 1891 — an episode that cost Freud real professional standing at the time. The drug's one lasting medical contribution, local anesthesia, went to his colleague Carl Koller instead, a missed priority Freud reportedly regretted for years.

What's genuinely contested is how much this shaped the theory itself, rather than just the man. Freud's cocaine use overlapped with the self-analysis — largely conducted through his 1890s correspondence with Wilhelm Fliess — that produced the Oedipus complex and the dream-interpretation method, and the specimen dream The Interpretation of Dreams builds its entire wish-fulfillment theory around, "Irma's injection," is Freud's own anxious reworking of a real medical misjudgment with clear echoes of the Fleischl-Marxow affair. Independent researchers, most persistently Peter Swales, have argued cocaine played a larger and longer role in this period than the official histories acknowledge, and that its mood-elevating, confidence-inflating effects are worth weighing against the sweeping, ambitious character of the theorizing it accompanied. That's an interpretive claim about a life, not a testable one about a brain — but it belongs next to the rebuttals above: whatever else explains Freud's early scientific judgment, cocaine is a documented part of the record, not a rumor.

The first major clinical fork off this system is Jung — libido widened beyond sexuality, a collective rather than only personal unconscious — treated in the next section, with Sabina Spielrein as the hinge the father–son story usually erases. The second, in London rather than Zurich, is Melanie Klein's object relations: play as the child's free association, inner objects from the start, and envy as a desire that attacks the good because it is good — not the preschool Oedipus complex as the master key.

Jung 1912–1961, break with Freud to death

collective unconsciousarchetypeindividuationNietzsche / Zarathustra

The first major clinical break inside Freud's own movement is not Lacan and not the later rebuttals — it is Carl Gustav Jung. Correspondence with Freud begins in 1906; for a few years Jung is the designated heir and president of the International Psychoanalytical Association. The public rupture crystallizes around Wandlungen und Symbole der Libido (1911–12; English as Psychology of the Unconscious): Jung widens libido from sexual drive into a more general psychic energy, reads myth and religion as positive psychic work rather than only disguised wish, and refuses the Oedipus complex as the master key to every formation. Freud answers in On the History of the Psycho-Analytic Movement (1914) by casting Jung as a deviant who could not stay inside psychoanalysis. Sonu Shamdasani's corrective still matters here: Jung is not merely a Freudian heretic whose whole system descends from Vienna — but against Freud he is the fork that keeps an unconscious while dropping repression-as-master-mechanism.

What replaces that mechanism is the collective unconscious: a layer of psychic life shared across individuals, not reducible to personal repression of childhood wishes, stocked with archetypes — inherited organizing forms (Mother, Shadow, Self, and so on) that show up in dreams, myths, and symptoms as variations on a type rather than as encrypted memories of one's own biography. Therapy's aim shifts from lifting repression toward individuation: becoming a more differentiated whole by integrating what the ego has cast as other — including the shadow, the disowned traits and potentials that return as projection onto others. That is a different clinical wager from Freud's, and a different metaphysics of what "unconscious" even means: teleological and mythopoietic rather than primarily archival and sexual.

The break is usually told as Freud versus Jung. Sabina Spielrein sits inside the same quarrel and is too often erased from it. A Russian-Jewish physician, Jung's early patient and then colleague, she presented "Destruction as the Cause of Coming Into Being" to the Vienna Psychoanalytic Society in 1912 — arguing that creation itself carries a destructive / transformative drive — eight years before Freud's own death-drive turn in Beyond the Pleasure Principle (1920). She worked the corridor between Zurich and Vienna while the personal alliance was collapsing; later historians (including those recovering her papers) treat her as an independent theorist, not a footnote to Freud or Jung. Without the Spielrein hinge, "Freud vs Jung" flattens into a father–son story that the archive never supported. Klein is the clinician who took that destruction-from-the-start seriously at the infant's body: annihilating anxiety in the first months, not a late philosophical add-on to the pleasure principle.

Where Freud ducked Nietzsche, Jung claimed him. The shared Schopenhauerian soil is real, but Jung's long engagement — culminating in the Zarathustra seminars of 1934–39 — treats Nietzsche's visionary text as a psychological case of inflation with an archetypal figure, read beside Jung's own Liber Novus / Red Book crisis rather than as a philosopher to cite politely. Drives, the self as contested multiplicity, the thinness of the rational ego: Jung takes the Nietzschean package Freud kept at arm's length and psychologizes it. That is why the Thinkers Timeline runs a build edge from Nietzsche to Jung as well as the critique edge from Freud.

The quarrel that matters is the 1912 fork, the Nietzsche debt, and — in the margin — the 1930s race/nation dossier that post-Jungians still have to repair, not mid-century Jungianism, New Age reception, or archetype-as-pop-brand. Klein, next, is the British clinical fork that still has to pass before Lacan. Mapping a substrate for the collective layer would fake a continuity the 1912 break does not license.

how this landed

Shamdasani — strongest corrective against the Freudocentric legend that treats Jung as only a psychoanalytic schismatic; still leaves the 1912 break load-bearing for anyone mapping depth psychology's forks.

Nietzsche — documented lifelong reading, not the anxious avoidance Freud practiced; the Zarathustra seminars are the mature statement, concurrent with Heidegger's Nietzsche lectures and with the same German catastrophe decade treated below.

Spielrein — recovered as theorist of destruction/transformation and as clinical hinge; still under-taught relative to Freud and Jung.

antisemitism / 1930s

Not a Nazi Party member — and not "exonerated" by having helped individual Jewish colleagues. Two tracks stay on the record at once.

Institutional — In 1933 Jung took the presidency of the (soon International) General Medical Society for Psychotherapy as the German field came under Nazi control. He restructured membership so Jews barred from the German national group could join as individuals, and claimed the motive was protective. Critics (and some defenders) note the same move also kept a Jungian presence inside German psychotherapy after Freud was banned and his books burned.

Doctrinal — the harder problem — Andrew Samuels's line: Jung's psychology of nations puts him in the same frame as antisemitic ideology even without being a collaborator. Hard texts, not rumors: "The Role of the Unconscious" (1918) contrasts Jewish "rootlessness" with Germanic chthonic excess; "The State of Psychotherapy Today" (1934) claims the "Aryan" unconscious has a "higher potential" than the Jewish, treats Jews as cultural nomads needing a host nation, warns against applying "Jewish categories" (Freud/Adler) as universal, and reads National Socialism as an eruption from the Germanic psyche Freud could not see. A 1933 Radio Berlin interview praises the Führer as organic leader of a people's movement. A Kranefeldt letter (1934) calls Freud and Adler's viewpoints specifically Jewish and "corrosive."

Change, not erasure — After Gustav Bally's 1934 Neue Zürcher Zeitung critique Jung grows more cautious; by the late 1930s–wartime years his public comments on Nazi Germany turn sharply negative (Schoenl & Peck). Late private remark to Sigmund Hurwitz: the 1934 essay was "nonsense." Post-Jungian critics (Samuels; Farhad Dalal's "Jung: A Racist") still argue the method — listing innate group traits for Jew, German, woman, and so on — needs reparation, not only the wartime optics. Jung's name appeared in Nazi-era bibliographies; that shows porosity, not that he wrote Nazi theory.

Keep both tracks — Jung on the clinical fork and the Nietzsche line; do not launder the 1930s texts into a personality dispute, and do not let them delete the conceptual break with Freud.

Klein · object relations 1921–1960, play technique to Envy and Gratitude

play techniqueparanoid-schizoid / depressiveprojective identificationenvy · reparationdeath instinct

Melanie Klein is the London fork after Jung's Zurich: not a collective unconscious and not yet Lacan's Symbolic, but the infant's inner objects from the first months. An Austrian-Jewish analyst, encouraged by Ferenczi in Budapest, she settled in London and made child analysis possible without waiting for a talking patient. Play technique treats the child's play as the equivalent of free association and the dream: toys, drawings, and enacted scenes are already symbolic traffic, interpretable in the session. The Psychoanalysis of Children (1932) is the method book; the 1946 paper "Notes on Some Schizoid Mechanisms" is the structural pin. The infant, on her account, already relates to a part-object — first the breast — as good or bad, and the psyche's early work is splitting, projection, and introjection rather than the later repression of a preschool Oedipal wish. Two positions name that work, not developmental stages you graduate from: the paranoid-schizoid position splits the object to manage annihilating anxiety; the depressive position integrates good and bad in one whole object, with guilt and a wish to repair. Projective identification is the mechanism that travels: unwanted parts of the self are forced into the other, who may then be lived as containing them — a two-person unconscious, not a one-person archive of wishes.

She is also the clinician who took Freud's death instinct at full strength. Spielrein's 1912 destruction paper and Freud's 1920 Beyond the Pleasure Principle stay programmatic until Klein dates annihilating anxiety to the first months: the infant's own aggression, projected, returns as persecution. Where Freud dates the Oedipus complex to roughly ages three to five — the child wanting one parent and rivalling the other as people in the house — Klein has those relations already running in the first year, as traffic with internal parental objects, not a household triangle you wait until nursery age to enter. Unconscious phantasy — the spelling with 'ph' is the British school's marker — is the mental expression of drive from the start, not a later decoration of need. Susan Isaacs's "The Nature and Function of Phantasy," written for the wartime Controversial Discussions, is the paper that made that claim the Kleinian line.

Envy and Gratitude (1957; given at the 1955 IPA congress) is the late load-bearing book. Envy is not greed for a missing object and not Kojève's desire for recognition: it is the attack on the good object because it is good — the breast that feeds is hated for having what the infant does not. Gratitude is the depressive-position capacity that can receive the good without having to spoil it. That is a theory of desire the sibling desire genealogy still needs beside recognition, mimesis, and desiring-production: wanting that destroys its source.

Hanna Segal, Klein's analysand and the clearest transmitter of the school in English, is the hinge that meets Lacan on symbol rather than on the breast. "Notes on Symbol Formation" (1957) distinguishes symbolic equation — the psychotic concrete collapse in which the symbol is the thing — from a true symbol, which requires the depressive position's capacity to tolerate absence and loss. Klein's own 1930 paper on symbol-formation in the ego, and the "Dick" case Lacan later savages in Seminar I as slamming symbolism onto a child "with complete brutality," sit behind that distinction. The quarrel with Lacan is therefore not only Imaginary-versus-signifier. Lacan praises Klein for keeping the death drive and the part-object (breast, feces, penis as clinical leftovers he will philosophize into objet a); he refuses her claim that object-relating precedes language. Kleinians read him as abandoning the infant's actual object-world for linguistics. Keep both charges; do not pick a winner.

That is why she belongs between Jung and Lacan. Jung kept an unconscious and dropped repression-as-master-mechanism for myth. Klein keeps drive and drops the late-Oedipal timetable for infant phantasy. Without her, "Freud then Lacan" erases the British school Lacan is arguing against. Juliet Mitchell is not Lacan-only on the desire page: she edited The Selected Melanie Klein (1986). Kristeva's Melanie Klein (2000; English 2001) is the later philosophical biography that treats Klein as the thinker who made the mother the source of thought itself, not only of feeding — a Kleinian weather under Kristeva's own semiotic, not a courtesy citation.

The full Controversial Discussions, Bion's later container/contained (a development of projective identification), and Winnicott's Independent "there is no such thing as a baby" are developments of the same British clinic, not a second monograph or a third box. Attachment research and infant intersubjectivity rhyme; they are not Klein cashed out in fMRI. Panksepp's PLAY system, below, is a mammalian affect circuit, not her play technique. The Freudian–Lacanian–affective spine still has to pass through that British clinic — so envy, reparation, and part-object stay live when Lacan and Mitchell get used downstream.

how this landed

Isaacs — Susan Isaacs's "The Nature and Function of Phantasy" (written 1943 for the Controversial Discussions; published 1948) is the Kleinian line on unconscious phantasy; named here, not a second box beside Klein.

Segal — Hanna Segal's 1957 symbol-formation paper is the British hinge that meets Lacan's Symbolic without becoming a linguistics. Timeline pin: 1957.

Anna Freud — the wartime Controversial Discussions split the British Society into Kleinian, Anna Freudian, and Independent training groups; Anna Freud's ego-psychology / defence analysis is the rival child clinic, not mapped here as a second box.

Winnicott · Bion — Independent "there is no such thing as a baby," and Bion's later container/contained, are named here and not boxed. Segal already carries the symbol quarrel with Lacan; boxing either as a third object-relations node would split the pin.

Horney — Karen Horney's culturalist dissent from Freud (womb envy, New Ways in Psychoanalysis, 1939) is a nearby fork; she sits in this margin so Klein remains the object-relations pin, not a stand-in for every feminist revision.

Lacan — Seminar I savages the "Dick" case and still praises Klein on the death drive and the part-object. Keep both charges; do not pick a winner.

Kristeva — Melanie Klein (2000; English 2001) is the philosophical biography, not a courtesy citation under the semiotic.

No substrate map — same rule as Jung's collective layer. Projective identification is a clinical description, not a circuit. Panksepp's PLAY, below, is a mammalian affect system, not her play technique.

The direct rebuttals 1927–2017, Malinowski to Crews

falsifiabilityclinical validationefficacy

Everything in the dissenters section later attacks whether any theory of subjectivity can be grounded in a physical substrate at all. This is a narrower and, for psychoanalysis specifically, more damaging line of attack: whether Freud's theory was ever good science on its own terms, independent of neuroscience entirely.

Karl Popper, developing his falsifiability criterion partly in direct reaction to psychoanalysis (alongside Marxism and Adlerian psychology) in 1920s Vienna, argued the theory's real problem wasn't being wrong but being unfalsifiable: it could accommodate any observation after the fact. His standard example contrasts a man who drowns a child and one who dies trying to save a child from drowning — Freudian theory, he said, could supply a confident explanation of either, from repression in one case to sublimation in the other, which is exactly the trouble: a theory that explains every possible outcome equally well explains none of them. On Popper's account, psychoanalysis isn't a false science — in the sense that matters, it isn't a science at all.

Adolf Grünbaum's The Foundations of Psychoanalysis (1984) is the more technical, and for defenders of Freud more uncomfortable, version of the critique, because he rejects Popper's unfalsifiability charge outright — psychoanalytic claims are testable in principle — and argues the theory fails on its own terms anyway. Freud's actual evidence was clinical: a patient's symptom relief after accepting an interpretation was supposed to show the interpretation was correct, what Freud called the tally argument. Grünbaum's point is that this can't distinguish a correct interpretation from the effect of suggestion, the analyst's authority, or the patient's motivation to agree — so the clinical evidence Freud actually offered never could have supported the theory, independent of any question about falsifiability.

Frederick Crews pushes past methodology into the historical record itself. The Memory Wars (1995) opened that archival assault on recovered-memory therapy and Freud's own case narratives; Freud: The Making of an Illusion (2017) is the late synthesis, documenting specific case histories where Freud's published account diverges sharply from what actually happened — Anna O., presented by Breuer and Freud as an early success, was institutionalized shortly after her supposed cure; the Wolf Man's claimed lifelong resolution didn't hold either. Crews's argument isn't just that the theory is weakly supported, it's that some of its founding evidence was retrospectively shaped, sometimes invented outright, to fit conclusions Freud had already reached.

Hans Eysenck asked a more practical question and got an unwelcome answer: does psychoanalytic therapy actually work? His 1952 survey argued patients in psychoanalysis recovered at rates no better than those given no treatment at all, spontaneous remission accounting for as much improvement as years on the couch. The finding has been contested and re-analyzed many times since, but it reframed the question from "is the theory true" to "does the practice built on it outperform doing nothing" — a much harder bar to clear than anyone in Freud's era assumed.

A narrower but sharp challenge came from anthropology. Bronisław Malinowski's fieldwork among the matrilineal Trobriand Islanders (Sex and Repression in Savage Society, 1927) found a father figure who wasn't the disciplinary authority Freud's Oedipal triangle requires — that role belonged to the mother's brother instead — and the rivalry and desire Freud predicted as universal didn't show up where the family structure it depends on wasn't there. If the Oedipus complex needs one particular kinship arrangement to arise at all, it's a claim about a culture's family structure, not a fact about the psyche as such. Mead, on the desire map, is the other Boasian cut: temperament and gendered wanting as cultural pattern. Girard later rewrites Oedipus as mimetic rivalry rather than innate incestuous desire — a different refusal of Freud's universal, from literature rather than the Trobriands.

how this landed

Popper — foundational in philosophy of science; most working psychologists would still cite falsifiability as the core problem with classical psychoanalytic claims.

Grünbaum — took Freud more seriously than most critics, which makes him harder to dismiss; widely regarded as the definitive technical critique.

Crews — some Freud scholars call it one-sided advocacy dressed as history, but its specific factual claims about individual cases have mostly held up under scrutiny.

Eysenck — his original numbers were disputed almost immediately; later meta-analyses (Smith & Glass, 1977) found psychotherapy in general does outperform no treatment, though psychoanalysis specifically remains one of the weaker performers in head-to-head comparisons.

Malinowski — anthropology broadly accepts the Oedipus complex, if it exists at all, isn't culturally universal in Freud's strong sense.

Lacan 1953–1981, seminars, three periods

Two encounters made Lacan a thinker the unconscious-as-desire argument has to wrestle with, and neither is the short mirror-stage essay that anthologies still use as a stand-in for the whole. The first is Freud: Lacan was training as a psychiatrist, not an analyst, when he decided the later psychoanalytic movement had blunted Freud's unconscious into ego-strengthening and normalization. The "return to Freud" is a return to that disruption — an unconscious with its own logic, irreducible to consciousness. Freud almost never uses desire; the German word is Wunsch, wish. Lacan reads the unconscious as unconscious desire anyway. The second encounter explains why. In the 1930s he sat, devotedly, in Alexandre Kojève's Hegel seminar — even arranging extra sessions at home — and once called Kojève his only master. Kojève's idiosyncratic amalgam of Hegel, Marx, and Heidegger made desire for recognition the motor of subjectivity. Put through a psychoanalytic system, that becomes the primacy of desire both this argument and the desire genealogy keep tracking. A third, quieter import is Kant: Lacan philosophizes psychoanalysis (the subject as a self-relating speaking being is a Kantian word Freud barely uses) and socializes it (Freud's castration becomes symbolic castration — lack as the price of being a speaking being, not a private sexual trauma). The linguistics below is how that subject gets built in a brain soaked in language. It is not, by itself, "what Lacan was up to."

How to read him follows from that. The Écrits (1966; the title just means "writings") are not a systematic book: commissioned prefaces, occasional essays, including the famous Kant-with-Sade piece, assembled haphazardly. The yearly seminars, from 1953, are the working-through. Almost every term changes meaning across that run without Lacan announcing the change — Jacques-Alain Miller later counted six distinct paradigms of enjoyment / jouissance alone — so dating a claim is part of understanding it. Mixing a Seminar III quilting point with a Seminar XXIII sinthome as if they occupy one Lacanian universe is a Frankenstein, not a reading. Todd McGowan's useful compass, which this section follows as a periodization rather than as a verdict: three main periods, coinciding roughly with the 1950s, 1960s, and 1970s, not five, and not one frozen RSI gadget.

The linguistics the signifier-unconscious claim needs starts with Ferdinand de Saussure's Course in General Linguistics (1916). Saussure splits the sign into signifier (sound-image) and signified (concept), insists the pairing is arbitrary — nothing about the sound "tree" binds it to the concept — and, more radically, that a sign carries no meaning alone: it means what it means only differentially, by not being every other sign in the system (language as "a system of differences with no positive terms"). Lacan pushes this further than Saussure did: he splits the sign apart and puts the signifier on top, bar over signified (S/s), arguing meaning isn't fixed by a signifier attaching to its signified but produced by signifiers sliding along a chain, only ever retroactively pinned down at nodal points he calls points de capiton (quilting points). That quilting-point idea is a Seminar III (psychosis) invention that then more or less drops out — one of the periodization's object lessons. Saussure lined signifier and signified up so a sentence could not come undone; Lacan treats the pairing as always about to slide, and the quilting point as the punctuation that temporarily stops it — retroactively, like Freud's Nachträglichkeit, deciding what the earlier words meant. A period at the end of "I want you to come here" is already a tiny version: congratulate you, or rob you. He also borrows Roman Jakobson's split of language into a metaphoric axis (substitution) and metonymic axis (combination) and maps it directly onto Freud's dream-work — condensation becomes a case of metaphor, displacement a case of metonymy — which is the actual technical spine underneath "the unconscious is structured like a language." Around the same time, Émile Benveniste's "Subjectivity in Language" (1958) argued personhood itself is a language-effect: pronouns like "I" are empty shifters — grammatical slots with no fixed referent — that become "the subject" only when someone actually speaks and occupies one. That's a direct source for Lacan's own split between the subject of the enunciation (whoever is speaking) and the subject of the enounced (the "I" inside what gets said): one more argument for why the subject is an effect of the signifying chain, not something already there that merely borrows language to express itself. A third structuralist import, Claude Lévi-Strauss's structural anthropology — kinship systems as a language of exchange, the incest taboo as the one universal law beneath all particular laws — feeds directly into Lacan's Symbolic order and the "Name-of-the-Father": the law isn't any actual father's prohibition, but a structural position that precedes and outlasts whoever happens to occupy it. Later the Name-of-the-Father is renamed the master signifier (S1): a signifier without a signified that starts a chain and orients a subject without itself meaning anything. Reception often treats S1 and quilting point as one word. McGowan's Cambridge Introduction to Jacques Lacan (2025) insists they do different jobs: S1 grounds (the empty start — "Aryan," "the market"); the quilting point punctuates (the period that retroactively tells you what that start meant — Marx's "class struggle" on a factory floor, "Jew" in Hitler's plot-story). Theory, on that reading, quilts a situation; it does not install a new S1. Keep both the date and the function when you use either. Klein's object-relations clinic, just above, is the British foil that sentence still needs: Lacan treats introjected objects as Imaginary, and the subject as an effect of the signifier rather than of the breast. Seminar I's assault on the "Dick" case — symbolism slammed onto a child "with complete brutality" — is that quarrel in the room; Segal's 1957 cut between symbolic equation and true symbol is the Kleinian answer that still has to meet the Symbolic rather than collapse into it.

Three registers do the work, and they map, more cleanly than most introductions admit, onto Kant's faculties. The Imaginary is what you see — image, rivalry, the lure of wholeness — akin to Kant's sensibility. The Symbolic is the position from which you see: parent, professor, citizen, all the identities that locate a gaze without exhausting what it finds — akin to the understanding's categories, which structure perception without being identical to the sense-data. The Real is the faculty that goes too far, like Kantian reason: early on, "what returns to the same place," the leftover that resists symbolic rearrangement (stars that keep coming back no matter how you rewrite the cosmology). Later it will not stay outside. A handy split inside the first two: the ideal-ego is an Imaginary image of the self one is not yet; the ego-ideal is a Symbolic point from which one imagines being watched and judged. The mirror stage — an infant, still uncoordinated, identifying with a unified specular image, a founding misrecognition (méconnaissance) — is the short text that got made to stand for all of this. It is not original to him, it is not the system's load-bearing invention, and its value is partly editorial: it is brief enough to anthologize. The developmental fact it leans on is real (visual system ahead of motor tract; see the margin). Treat it as an entry to the Imaginary, not as "Lacan."

McGowan's three periods, dated to the seminars rather than to the Écrits. Early (Seminars I–VI, 1950s) — the most Kantian stretch, apogee in Seminar II: the Symbolic has primacy; the Imaginary constantly deceives; the Real barely appears except as what returns to the same place, or in the ordinary sense of what one runs up against. Seminar VII, The Ethics of Psychoanalysis, is a hapax — "the only thing one can be guilty of is having given ground relative to one's desire," with Antigone as the example — a Kantian ethic in spirit even though Kant's own ethic has nothing to say about desire, then the break. Middle (Seminars VIII–XVI, 1960s) — Hegelian, and the stretch McGowan treats as the radical core. Seminar IX (Identification, 1961–62) introduces objet a (object a, objet petit a: same thing). Any "small a" before that seminar is not this concept. The object is not the object of desire; it is the obstacle that makes the object of desire desirable — the packaging you tear through and throw away, the crinkle on the page of a new book, the defect in a partner without which desire would evacuate. It is structurally close to waste, even feces; Lacan spends Seminar XV (The Psychoanalytic Act) on that link. Desire is no longer Kojève's desire for recognition; it is desire for the thing that slightly mars perfection and thereby keeps a perfect object seeming to exist just beyond reach. The Real, here, is not outside the Symbolic: it is an internal torsion, the point of contradiction that distorts the signifying structure from within. Seminar XVI (From an Other to the other) develops surplus enjoyment as a way to think capitalism — and then leaves it hanging. Lacan himself, looking back in Seminar XXI, called objet a what he invented. Late (Seminar XVII onward, 1970s) — four discourses as ways of relating inside the Symbolic (analyst's discourse set as the one that somehow avoids the master's place); then Seminars XIX–XX and the formulas of sexuation: masculine and feminine as a non-relation, "there is no sexual relation." No shared master signifier holding a chain of subjects together; a few years later the sinthome (Seminar XXIII, via Joyce) is a privileged symptom that cannot be interpreted away, only used to hold a singular subject together, more monad than link in a chain. McGowan reads this late turn as a retreat from Hegelian contradiction into Kantian multiplicity and non-relation — a preference, not a fact about the seminars. Copjec and Zupančič, next section, find the political charge of those same formulas; that quarrel stays open: Copjec and Zupančič against McGowan's ranking.

What objet a adds is a leftover that is not a thing in the world and not a brain module. It is the structural remainder produced once a speaking being is in language: not what you lost, but the gap the Symbolic creates and desire chases by mistaking the obstacle for a missing object. The clinical ancestor it philosophizes is Klein's part-object — breast, feces, the waste of relating — which Lacan keeps as leftover and refuses as an introjected thing that precedes language. Date both claims: Klein's part-object is 1930s–46 clinic; objet a is Seminar IX. Johnston and Žižek will try to say how a nervous system could support a gap like that. The concept itself only exists from Seminar IX; do not back-project it onto the 1950s RSI lectures, the mirror-stage essay, or Klein's breast.

Julia Kristeva, trained as a linguist before turning to psychoanalysis, pushes back on how purely linguistic this whole picture is. Her Revolution in Poetic Language (1974) proposes a semiotic register underneath the Symbolic: a rhythmic, bodily, drive-charged organization of pre-verbal sound and movement, tied to the infant's early relation to the maternal body, that never disappears once language arrives but keeps erupting through it — in rhythm, tone, poetic excess, the texture of a voice under what it says. That maternal-body weather is already Kleinian: Melanie Klein (2000; English 2001) is the later book that treats Klein as the thinker who made the mother the source of thought, not only of feeding — matricide as the condition of becoming a speaking subject. Where Lacan's Symbolic can sound like pure structure, all law and signifier, Kristeva's semiotic reinserts exactly the drive and the body the substrate argument keeps trying to locate physically — closer to Freud's primary process, and to Klein's unconscious phantasy, than to anything Saussure, Jakobson, or Benveniste describe on their own. Luce Irigaray, on the desire genealogy, presses a related but sharper charge: that the Symbolic itself is phallocentric, not merely missing a semiotic underside.

Two later linguists push back on how much of this is really arbitrary and built from exposure alone. Noam Chomsky's Universal Grammar argues the deep syntactic engine is substantially innate — Saussure's arbitrariness covers vocabulary (which sound means "tree"), not the grammar wrapping around it, which on Chomsky's account the brain arrives already expecting. George Lakoff pushes from the opposite direction: his conceptual metaphor theory (with Mark Johnson, later formalized with Jerome Feldman as an explicit "Neural Theory of Language") argues huge swaths of abstract language aren't arbitrary at all but systematically motivated by embodied, sensorimotor schemas — "argument is war," "more is up" — bodily patterns laid down before language arrives and later recruited to structure abstract thought, a concrete linguistics-side version of the same embodiment claim Damasio and the bodily-ego thread make below. Between the two of them, the perisylvian network isn't just passively built by exposure to arbitrary signs: it may arrive with real innate structure already expected (Chomsky), and it recruits pre-existing bodily circuitry to do abstract work (Lakoff) — exposure alone is doing less of the job than "arbitrariness" on its own suggests.

That is Chomsky the linguist. There is a second Chomsky, and the two must not be collapsed. In a December 2012 Veterans Unplugged interview — and already in a 1995 letter that called Lacan "an amusing and perfectly self-conscious charlatan" whose pre-cult work was "sensible" — he treats Paris "theory" as posturing: fancy terms, no principles from which you can deduce empirically testable propositions, nothing that survives decoding beyond what you could tell a twelve-year-old in five minutes. Žižek, in that interview, is the extreme. Lacan he actually knew, liked, and still thought was performing for the cameras. Alan Sokal and Jean Bricmont's Fashionable Nonsense (1997; Impostures intellectuelles) is a different knife on the same body: not "no theory" but the late seminars' topology and mathemes — torus as neurotic subject, Klein bottle, square root of minus one as erectile organ — treated as if they were arguments rather than costume. That charge is strongest on the 1970s knots. It does not automatically delete the 1950s Saussure–Jakobson spine or Seminar IX's leftover. Dating the claim, refusing the Frankenstein, and refusing objet a a brain module is already a partial concession: if a sentence cannot be stated once the polysyllables are stripped, it is not doing work here. What survives decoding (quilting as punctuation, S1 as empty start, objet a as obstacle) is still not a scientific theory in Chomsky's sense. Johnston, next, is the one who tries to make a gap like that a property of nature rather than a flourish.

how to date him

Seminars, not Écrits — the written collection is not a compendium of the system; work the yearly seminars. English has official Seuil/Polity volumes plus Cormac Gallagher's complete unofficial set. Intentional obscurity (Freud was "too clear") produced as many mystifications as it prevented.

Terms move — quilting point, Name-of-the-Father / phallus, jouissance, even the Real: meanings shift and sometimes drop. "Subject" is the rare near-constant (thinner in the very late seminars). Miller's "six paradigms of jouissance" is the chronicle; Lacan never flags the redefinitions himself.

McGowan — three-period Kant → Hegel → Kant-again reading is a strong compass, not the only one. His lament of the late period is a Hegelian preference; Copjec and Zupančič, next, keep the formulas of sex as politically live. The same 2025 introduction distinguishes master signifier (grounds, no signified) from quilting point (punctuation). Reception usually collapses them; keep the split.

substrate proposals

arbitrariness of the sign — no innate wiring ties a phoneme to a meaning; the perisylvian network has to build that mapping entirely from exposure, which is exactly what Saussure's "arbitrary" is pointing at.

mirror stage — rests on a real developmental fact: infant visual system matures faster than the corticospinal tract, so the mirror really does show a coherence the body doesn't yet have. That does not make the 1949 essay the center of the system; anthologies did that.

Visual cortex (calcarine/V1) calcarine sulcus visible ~wk 16, among the earliest; acuity nears adult levels by 6–12mo — far ahead of the motor tract above

Symbolic order — language acquisition physically remodels the left perisylvian network: the ring of cortex around the Sylvian (lateral) fissure, comprising Broca's area (inferior frontal gyrus — syntax, production), Wernicke's area (posterior superior temporal gyrus — comprehension, semantics), and the arcuate fasciculus, the white-matter tract linking them that underwrites the phonological loop (holding a heard string of sound long enough to repeat or parse it). Left-lateralized in roughly 95% of right-handers, but that lateralization itself is built developmentally, not fixed at birth — late-first-language cases (congenitally deaf children denied early sign exposure, the "Genie" case) show the window genuinely closes, leaving permanent syntactic deficits even once exposure resumes. linguistics, qualified

Benveniste — shifters (pronouns like "I") are a real, well-documented grammatical category; whether they're the mechanism of subjectivity or just its grammatical trace is the contestable part.

Kristeva — the semiotic/symbolic split is influential in literary theory, but "drive-charged rhythm" has no agreed neural signature; treat as clinical-phenomenological, not a measured claim.

Chomsky — two jobs. Universal Grammar vs Saussure is a minority nativist linguistics claim; some innate syntactic bias remains widely accepted. The 2012 / 1995 "no theory" charge is a different bar: empirically testable propositions, not rival grammar. The substrate bet partly shares it (no module for objet a) and partly refuses it (dated concepts can still be philosophy).

Sokal · Bricmont — 1996 Social Text hoax, then the 1997 book. Targets Lacan's math, not the quilting-point sentence. Do not use them to skip dating the seminars.

Lakoff — conceptual metaphor theory is well evidenced behaviorally; the Neural Theory of Language project's specific circuit claims are far more speculative than the behavioral core.

Žižek, Copjec, Johnston 1989–present, the philosophical leap

Hegel + Lacanformulas of sextranscendental materialism

A key date in the English-language — and not only English — understanding of what Lacan was for philosophy is 1989. Slavoj Žižek's The Sublime Object of Ideology and Joan Copjec's essay "The Orthopsychic Subject" (then Read My Desire, 1994) are the pair that made the seminars usable as philosophy rather than as a mirror-stage gadget plus linguistics. Early introductions had systematically underestimated that. This section keeps both names at the start so the leap is not Žižek alone.

Joan Copjec is the Kantian of the pair. Read My Desire: Lacan Against the Historicists (1994) argues that reducing sex, look, and subject to historical discourse — the Foucault-inflected move that also feeds Butler — misses what Lacan is doing with a limit that cannot be historicized away. The last chapter, "Sex and the Euthanasia of Reason," is the essay to have written on the formulas of sexuation (Seminar XX, Encore): she maps masculine and feminine as a non-relation onto the Kantian antinomies of pure reason — two equally necessary, mutually uncompletable ways reason overruns what it can know. That is why the late seminars' "there is no sexual relation" fits a return to Kant so well, and why a Hegelian reader like McGowan can lament the same stretch Copjec makes her strongest. She is the other half of why 1989 changed the map. Irigaray presses sexual difference as a morphology the Symbolic cannot count; Copjec presses it as a logical deadlock. Adjacent fights, different machines.

Žižek folds Lacan back into Hegel: the subject isn't a positive thing but a self-relating negativity, a gap in nature rather than an addition to it — Hegel's "night of the world." Ideology, in this reading, isn't false belief covering a real world; it's a fantasy that structures what counts as reality in the first place, organized around a traumatic Real it can never fully symbolize. The classic example in For They Know Not What They Do is Hitler quilting every misery as a Jewish plot: that is punctuation, not the Nazi master signifier, which has to be the empty start (Deutschland, Aryan) the scapegoat is there to make mean something. McGowan's split is why those two jobs should not share a name. Chomsky's 2012 dismissal of Žižek as empty posturing, and Žižek's improvised Birkbeck reply that Chomsky does not know what continental theory is for, is the same fight from the other chair: the leap is not a scientific program. Johnston is the exception who tries to make it one. The parallax view pushes this further: the truth of an object is the irreducible shift between two incompatible perspectives on it, not a third view that reconciles them. That Hegelian Lacan is, in McGowan's periodization, a reading of the middle seminars' objet a and internal Real, even when Žižek also raids the late formulas. The surplus enjoyment Seminar XVI named and dropped is one of the capitalist tools this reception actually develops.

Alenka Zupančič, with Žižek and Mladen Dolar in the Ljubljana school, takes the formulas of sexuation as a political ontology, not a retreat into monads. Ethics of the Real (2000) and later What Is Sex? (2017) treat the feminine side especially as a way to think a non-all that does not collapse into a particular identity. McGowan notes the power and still prefers the middle-period object; the honest move is to keep both. Mari Ruti's work on the sinthome as a singular guide rather than a master signifier is a related late-Lacan retrieval that has to fiddle with the theory to make it livable — flag it, don't pretend Lacan already wrote that ethics.

The parallax move is the interesting stress-test for this whole project. If first-person (analytic) and third-person (neuroscientific) descriptions of a subject are a genuine parallax gap, no amount of neuroscience closes it by adding detail — which would mean this entire exercise has a hard ceiling. Adrian Johnston takes the bet anyway.

Johnston's transcendental materialism

Johnston's own name for the project — already in play in Žižek's Ontology (2008), then laid out across the planned three-volume Prolegomena to Any Future Materialism (vols. 1–2, 2013 and 2019; vol. 3 forthcoming) — borrows "transcendental" in Kant's sense: not transcendent, not something beyond nature, but the conditions that make a subject possible in the first place. His bet is that those conditions can be found entirely inside nature, provided nature is understood correctly — which for Johnston means nature is not the seamless, fully self-consistent totality both hard determinists and vulgar materialists assume it is. He calls this weak nature: a nature riddled with genuine gaps, contingencies, and self-inconsistencies rather than one unified deterministic block — a materialist rewrite of Žižek's Hegelian claim that Substance is already, in itself, split. On this account a free, self-relating subject doesn't have to be smuggled in from outside physical reality; it can emerge from cracks already present in nature's own structure.

He backs this with actual biology rather than leaving it purely speculative: neural plasticity (the brain's wiring is substantially built by experience, not fixed in advance — a resource Johnston and Malabou draw on in Self and Emotional Life (2013), engaging Damasio, LeDoux, and Panksepp rather than treating them as targets to refute), degeneracy in neural systems (many different circuit configurations can produce the same output, so there's no single fixed hardware-to-function mapping to be determined by), and epigenetics (gene expression is environmentally modulated, not a fixed blueprint). Together these give a technical, non-mystical version of Freud's old drive/instinct distinction: Freud's own Trieb/Instinkt distinction turns on variable aim and object rather than fixed animal instinct; Lacan then names the bending mechanism the signifier. Johnston's claim is that the protracted window of human neural plasticity is literally what makes that Lacanian denaturing possible: the same developmental immaturity that lets language remodel the perisylvian network (above) is also what lets culture and the signifier partially decouple drive from fixed biological instinct — nature, using only natural means, producing something that outruns pure natural determination.

how this landed

Copjec — "The Orthopsychic Subject" (October, 1989) then Read My Desire (1994). The sexuation chapter is still the essay to beat; she is a Kantian reader, which is why Seminar XX is her home and why McGowan's Hegelian ranking of periods will not be hers.

Zupančič — Ljubljana school, not a Žižek satellite. What Is Sex? (2017) is the later public pin; Ethics of the Real (2000) is the timeline pin. Political reading of the feminine "non-all," not a mirror-stage intro.

substrate proposals

Johnston draws directly on Panksepp, LeDoux, and Damasio to argue the gap Žižek describes is a real property of a brain whose circuits get symbolically overwritten during development, not a metaphor.

weak nature — leans on neural degeneracy (many wirings, one function) and epigenetics (expression set by environment) as biology's own evidence against a fully closed, deterministic substrate.

Status: philosophical argument built from neuroscience, not a testable neuroscience claim in itself. Copjec and Zupančič are not in the substrate business; they are why Lacan here is dated and philosophical rather than a 1949 anthology piece.

Hofstadter treats the self as a strange loop: not a substance anywhere in the brain, but a pattern that appears when a system represents itself representing things, recursively, until a stable "I" seems to fall out of the swirl. It's a good account of the ego as representation — a something-from-nothing story compatible with Lacan's ego-as-misrecognition — but it's silent on drives, affect, or the id; Hofstadter's loop runs on symbols, not hunger or fear. On the consciousness map the loop is the cyan “nothing extra” node.

John Searle's Chinese Room (1980) is the specific problem with leaving it at that. A person who speaks no Chinese sits in a room with a rulebook, receives Chinese characters through a slot, follows purely syntactic instructions for which characters to send back, and produces replies indistinguishable from a fluent speaker's — without understanding a single word. Searle's point: formal symbol manipulation, however complex or recursive, is syntax; understanding is semantics, and no amount of syntax on its own adds up to semantics. Run that against Hofstadter directly: a strange loop is exactly this kind of recursive symbol-shuffling, so the Chinese Room asks whether it gives you the pattern of a self without whatever it is that makes a self actually understand anything. Searle isn't a dualist about this — his own label is biological naturalism: he thinks consciousness is caused by specific biological processes, just not by formal computation alone, wherever it runs — which makes him a third position, distinct from both the grounding project above and the property-dualist dissent below. On the consciousness map he is the biological-naturalism violet node.

The other way out of Descartes besides Searle's biological naturalism is not a better inner juice but the refusal of an inner room. Francisco Varela (Chilean neurobiologist), Evan Thompson, and Eleanor Rosch's The Embodied Mind (1991) treats consciousness as sense-making by a living, world-coupled body — autopoiesis meeting the phenomenological tradition that Genealogies parks at Merleau-Ponty and does not follow further. Varela's later neurophenomenology asks first-person method to meet third-person data rather than wait for the hard problem to dissolve. On the consciousness map this is the left-of-centre enactive node: a silicon replay of the neural syntax without the organism–world loop would miss the thing. Rosch is not a courtesy name — The Embodied Mind is a three-author book, and her categories-of-cognition work is why the enactive current is not another all-male lab.

Churchland (Paul and Patricia) is the deliberate skeptic to keep in the room. Eliminative materialism's core warning: folk-psychological categories sometimes don't get reduced to neuroscience, they get replaced by it — the way "caloric fluid" wasn't found in thermodynamics, it was discarded. Applied here: id/ego/superego might be pre-scientific placeholders a mature neuroscience eventually drops rather than locates. Patricia Churchland's own account of conscience — oxytocin/vasopressin circuits, mirror-neuron systems, prefrontal-limbic interaction shaped by attachment and culture — is a real alternative to Freud's internalized-parent story, arriving at a similar developmental timeline by a much more chemical route. On the consciousness map eliminativism is the cyan “folk psychology discarded” node.

Damasio distinguishes a moment-to-moment core self (brainstem/insular homeostatic feeling) from a narrative autobiographical self built from memory — a fairly literal cashing-out of Freud's bodily-ego footnote.

Friston, often working directly with Solms, models the brain as a prediction machine minimizing surprise between top-down generative models and bottom-up sensory/interoceptive signal. This gives mechanistic readings of several constructs at once: repression as actively down-weighting certain prediction errors so they never reach awareness; ideology-as-fantasy as a generative model imposed from above that determines what will even register as "real"; the Real as the residue no revision of the model can absorb. Anil Seth's Being You (2021) popularizes the same architecture as a “beast machine”: you are a controlled hallucination in the service of staying alive, with interoception — not just vision — as the root of selfhood. That cluster now has its own cyan node on the consciousness map: computable, mechanism-suffices. Lisa Feldman Barrett's constructed-emotion work is the affective companion, not a side-quest — feelings as predictions about the body's energy budget rather than Darwinian stamp-modules, which is why a predictive account of the unconscious cannot stay a Friston–Solms-only story. She now has her own map node beside Seth, not a buried credit in his thesis. (See also predictive processing.)

LeDoux gives the cleanest hard evidence for an unconscious in the weak sense: the amygdala's fast subcortical threat pathway processes and triggers action before, and without, conscious representation — though LeDoux himself resists stretching this circuit-level finding to cover the rich, meaning-laden Freudian unconscious. Malabou pushes back on the static structuralism in Lacan by insisting the brain is plastic in the strong sense — capable of receiving form and exploding it — reframing subject-formation as continual, revisable sculpting rather than a fixed architecture laid down once in infancy.

Gerald Edelman's Neural Darwinism (1987) — the Theory of Neuronal Group Selection (TNGS) — gives a developmental mechanism underneath a lot of what's asserted above, rather than another endpoint description. Development doesn't wire the brain to a genetic blueprint; it overproduces wildly variable neuronal groups and connections (a primary repertoire), and experience then selects and strengthens some pathways over others through reentry — massively parallel, recursive signaling between otherwise separate brain maps that binds them into one coherent scene with no central observer reading the result. Edelman later split consciousness itself into two kinds: primary consciousness, a present-tense perceptual "remembered present" available to many animals, and higher-order consciousness, which requires the semantic and linguistic capacity to model a self across past and future. That split lines up cleanly with Freud's primary/secondary process distinction above, and even better with the claim that language acquisition is what promotes a Symbolic-order self out of what was, until then, just felt in the present. It's also the most rigorous version of the plasticity argument Malabou gestures at — selection among an already-diverse repertoire, not sculpting of a blank slate — and gives Hofstadter's strange loop an actual candidate mechanism: reentry is recursive, self-referential signaling implemented in real neuroanatomy, not just a computational metaphor.

Where Edelman, Malabou, and Johnston actually connect

These three aren't just thematically similar — two of the links are citational, not just an analogy drawn for the substrate bet. Johnston's degeneracy argument for weak nature isn't borrowed loosely from biology in general; it's Edelman's own term. Edelman and immunologist Joseph Gally extended "degeneracy" — a term already used in genetics and immunology — to nervous systems in a 2001 paper, naming something selectionist theories need and rigid-wiring theories don't: structurally different elements — different neuronal groups, different pathways — able to produce the same functional output. It's what makes selection possible as a matter of principle: a system with only one way to do each thing has nothing left for experience to select among. Johnston takes that degeneracy directly from Edelman's neuroscience (alongside plasticity and epigenetics) as one of his three empirical planks for weak nature — the same reentrant, selectionist architecture Edelman built to explain a unified conscious scene emerging from a brain with no fixed wiring diagram is, for Johnston, exactly the "underdetermination of brain by brain" that leaves room for a self-relating subject.

Malabou is where it gets contentious rather than cumulative. Johnston's own Prolegomena project stages its first volume explicitly against Lacan, Badiou, and Meillassoux. His disagreement with Malabou — developed especially around Self and Emotional Life and related essays — treats her account of plasticity (the brain able to receive form, give form, and explode form) as necessary but not sufficient. A system that's merely plastic, endlessly reshaped by whatever experience impresses on it, is still just a very flexible natural mechanism; flexibility alone doesn't get you a subject in the Hegelian-Lacanian sense — something that relates negatively to itself, not just adaptively to its inputs. Johnston wants the gap Žižek describes layered on top of Malabou's plasticity and Edelman's degeneracy, not derived from them: the biology explains how the hardware could support a subject, not why a subject, rather than just a very adaptive nervous system, actually shows up.

The Badiou Johnston is staging against is a rival materialism of the subject, not a straw man. Alain Badiou's Being and Event (1988) treats ontology as mathematics (set theory), and the subject as what is produced by fidelity to an event — a rupture that cannot be deduced from the situation that precedes it (love, politics, science, art as the four truth-procedures). That subject is not a Freudian drive-economy lodged in weak nature; it is a local operator of truth after the event. Johnston's transcendental materialism refuses to let the subject float free of a biologically underdetermined nervous system in that way — and refuses Meillassoux's related speculative-realist move of absolutizing contingency outside any subject of experience. Badiou stays as the foil that clarifies what Johnston is not doing, rather than as a full alternative ontology to develop here.

The whole selectionist-plasticity family also has a more direct ancestor than any of the three credit enough: Freud's own abandoned Project for a Scientific Psychology (1895). Before he gave up on neurology for psychology, Freud modeled the mind as neurons passing a "quantity" of excitation through pathways of varying resistance — facilitation (Bahnung) — where repeated passage lowers resistance and experience literally carves the network's future traffic, not by rewiring to a plan but by wearing some paths in over others. That's a plastic, selectionist model of learning built from almost nothing, decades before Edelman and roughly a century before Malabou — Freud simply didn't have the neuroscience to make it stick, and dropped it for the psychical apparatus instead.

Malabou, for her part, turns this back on Freud rather than just extending him. The New Wounded (2007) argues classical psychoanalysis — for all that it started in Freud's own neurology — built its theory of trauma entirely around repressed psychical conflict, with no real category for a purely organic wound: a stroke, a lesion, dementia, that produces an unrecognizable new person with no repressed content to interpret at all. Her "destructive plasticity" is explicitly a complaint that psychoanalysis undersells the brain's material capacity to just break a self rather than symbolize one — which cuts against Johnston's move to fold biological plasticity in as support for the Freudian drive story, rather than a challenge to it.

Put together, the throughline runs: Freud's drive is already an instinct with variable aim and object; Lacan then names the bending mechanism the signifier. Johnston wants to know how a physical nervous system could be bent that way at all without smuggling in magic, and reaches for exactly the resources the substrate argument needed for other reasons anyway — Edelman's degeneracy, generic neural plasticity — as the material precondition. Malabou supplies much of the same biology aimed in closer to the opposite direction, using it to show where Freud's framework runs out rather than where it gets confirmed. None of the three quite agree with each other, which is the useful part: it's a live disagreement about how much of "the subject" biology can actually carry, conducted by people who take both the biology and the subject seriously.

Dreams: a case study 1977–present, activation-synthesis to today

activation-synthesisSEEKING vs. brainstem noisethreat / social simulationcontinuity hypothesis

Dreams are the one place Freud made a falsifiable, mechanism-level claim — that dreams are meaningful products of unconscious wish and drive, not noise — and it's the one place the fight over that claim has actually been had in public, with real data on both sides, instead of staying philosophical. The 1900 mechanism is tighter than "dreams mean something": a dream is the disguised fulfillment of a repressed wish; dream-work (condensation, displacement, secondary revision) hides the wish so sleep can continue. He already qualified the universal in 1920: traumatic dreams in Beyond the Pleasure Principle repeat the unassimilated event rather than fulfill a wish. Jung, above, had already dropped disguise for compensation: the dream portrays the situation in the unconscious rather than encrypting a Wunsch. The scientific fight below is therefore not wish versus noise as the only two options.

J. Allan Hobson and Robert McCarley's activation-synthesis hypothesis (1977) was the direct neuroscientific rebuttal. During REM sleep the pons fires largely random bursts of activity — PGO waves (ponto-geniculo-occipital, named for the path they take from pons to lateral geniculate nucleus to occipital cortex) — while the brain's aminergic systems (serotonin, norepinephrine) go quiet and cholinergic tone rises. On this account a dream is the forebrain's best-effort narrative synthesis, imposed after the fact on essentially meaningless bottom-up noise: a confabulation, not a disguised wish. Hobson spent much of his career as psychoanalytic dream theory's most public scientific critic on exactly this basis.

The circuitry Solms leans on has a name of its own. Jaak Panksepp's Affective Neuroscience (1998) maps seven primary mammalian affect systems — SEEKING, RAGE, FEAR, LUST, CARE, PANIC/GRIEF, PLAY — onto conserved subcortical circuitry (hypothalamus, PAG, extended amygdala and their ascending projections), arguing these are not cognitive appraisals of feeling but evolved, felt motivational engines. PLAY here is a conserved mammalian affect circuit, not Klein's play technique (the child's session as free association). SEEKING is the general appetitive / exploratory drive: dopaminergic, future-oriented, the urge to go looking. Solms's wager — and Johnston's later biological reading of the id — is that Freud's drive vocabulary is not a metaphor floating above that map but a clinical name for roughly these systems, with SEEKING the closest empirical cashing-out of the id's wanting.

Mark Solms's clinical data cuts the other way. Patients with lesions to the pontine brainstem structures Hobson's model treats as necessary for dreaming can still dream normally; patients with damage instead to the ventromesial frontal white matter carrying dopaminergic projections from the mesolimbic SEEKING system — the same circuit already doing the id's work above — report a complete, selective loss of dreaming, with REM physiology otherwise intact. Dreaming also turns up in NREM sleep far more often than the classic REM-only picture allows. Solms's conclusion: dreaming isn't generated by the brainstem machinery Hobson pointed to at all — it's generated, or at least permitted, by the same appetitive system psychoanalysis already called the engine of desire. That is the closest empirical win for a Freudian claim in this argument — if the claim is that dreaming is permitted by an appetitive drive system, not that a latent infantile wish has been decrypted. SEEKING online in sleep is compatible with Jungian compensation, with Cartwright's mood work, and with associative exploration; it does not, by itself, restore dream-censorship.

Antti Revonsuo's threat simulation theory (2000) is the evolutionary foil that keeps dreams organized while dropping the wish. Dreams, on this account, over-represent danger as cheap offline rehearsal of threat perception and avoidance; with Katja Valli and Jarno Tuominen the same program later adds social simulation — interpersonal scenes as practice for coalition, conflict, and bonding, not a hinterworld of wishes. Antonio Zadra and Robert Stickgold's NEXTUP (Network Exploration to Understand Possibilities, 2021) is a different job for the same organized-not-a-wish slot: REM explores weak associations across memory so knowledge can generalize; the dream is the experience of that search. Rosalind Cartwright's mood-regulation findings and Matthew Walker's "overnight therapy" — REM depotentiating affective charge while keeping the memory — sit next to that: affect processing of current stress, not infantile disguise. Owen Flanagan's spandrel argument (Dreaming Souls, 2000) and Crick and Mitchison's reverse-learning ("we dream to forget," 1983) are the peer claim that even organized phenomenology might have no interpretive job at all. (Francis Crick's NCC/claustrum work on the consciousness map is a different Crick claim.) None of these restore Hobson's pontine confabulation, and none of them need Freud's dream-work.

G. William Domhoff's neurocognitive theory is still the deflationary account of content: dreaming correlates most reliably with default-mode network activity — the same medial-prefrontal/posterior-cingulate system already implicated in mentalizing and the ego-ideal — and its content mostly continues waking concerns (the continuity hypothesis) rather than encoding a disguised wish or synthesizing random noise. Continuity can be true of what dreams look like while Revonsuo and NEXTUP still fight over why the brain bothers. On this view, dreaming is less a royal road to the unconscious than a side effect of a self-modeling network that never fully switches off.

Hobson vs. Solms was an open, often heated dispute through the 1990s–2000s. Solms's dissociation of dreaming from REM and his forebrain/dopaminergic account are widely influential; his further identification of that circuitry with Panksepp's SEEKING system and Freudian wish-fulfillment remains contested. Many researchers keep Hobson's REM neurophysiology (PGO waves, the aminergic-cholinergic switch) as an accurate description of REM sleep itself — just not proof dreaming is meaningless. Revonsuo's threat/social simulation is a live evolutionary research program: organized dreaming without disguise. NEXTUP is newer and more memory-computational. The dissenters 1995–present, the hard-problem revival

hard problemMary's Roompanpsychism / idealismanti-neuro Lacan

Everything above assumes the project is coherent — that a construct like the id or the ego-ideal, given enough neuroscience, either reduces to or at least correlates cleanly with something physical. That assumption has serious modern opponents on two fronts at once: philosophy of mind, arguing consciousness resists this kind of grounding no matter how detailed the science gets, and psychoanalysis's own orthodoxy, arguing the subject Lacan describes was never the kind of thing biology could find in the first place.

David Chalmers gave the modern version its name in “Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness” (1995), expanded in The Conscious Mind (1996): the hard problem of consciousness. Easy problems — how the brain discriminates stimuli, integrates information, reports its own states — are functional, and functional problems yield to mechanism, answerable in principle by more neuroscience. The hard problem is different in kind: why any of that functioning is accompanied by subjective experience at all, why there's something it is like to be the system doing it. His zombie argument presses the point — a creature physically identical to you, matching every behavior and every brain state, but with no inner experience, is conceivable without contradiction, which means facts about experience aren't logically fixed by physical facts alone. His own conclusion is a form of property dualism: phenomenal properties are additional, fundamental features of reality, not the kind of thing a sufficiently complete wiring diagram could ever entail — a late heir to the Cartesian mind–body gap, without Descartes's two substances. Run that against this whole document: even a perfect map of Panksepp's SEEKING circuit would give the full functional profile of wanting, and still leave open why wanting feels like anything. On the consciousness map he is the cyan node that keeps both simulation and the hard problem.

Frank Jackson's knowledge argument — Mary's Room (1982) — makes a related but distinct case, from evidence rather than conceivability. Mary is a brilliant scientist who has lived her whole life in a black-and-white room and learned, from books and screens, every physical fact there is about color and color vision — every wavelength, every fact about cones and V1 and neural processing. Then she walks outside and sees a red tomato for the first time. Jackson's claim: she learns something, what red actually looks like, that no amount of physical information could have given her — which means the phenomenal facts aren't included in the complete physical facts after all, contra physicalism. Worth the honesty of noting Jackson himself later recanted, in "Mind and Illusion" (2003), and went back to physicalism, arguing Mary gains a new representational ability rather than new propositional knowledge — which makes the knowledge argument a rare case in this whole document where the original author no longer holds the dissenting position, though most of the field still treats it as live.

Thomas Nagel — already well known for asking what it is like to be a bat — pushed further in Mind and Cosmos (2012), arguing reductive neo-Darwinian materialism is very likely an incomplete picture of nature, because it can't account for consciousness, reason, or value without quietly assuming what it's meant to explain. His alternative gestures at nature having some as-yet-unspecified teleological tendency toward generating conscious, rational beings — a claim most working biologists reject outright, but one of the highest-profile "materialism doesn't cover everything" arguments from inside analytic philosophy rather than from religion or continental theory. On the consciousness map he is the original hard-problem node.

Galen Strawson and Philip Goff take the opposite tack: instead of treating experience as a strange extra that emergent complexity somehow produces from wholly insentient matter, panpsychism proposes some primitive form of experience is already a basic, ubiquitous feature of matter itself — physics was never in the business of ruling consciousness out, it just never described it. The cost is the "combination problem": how billions of micro-experiences compose into one unified subject is exactly as hard as the problem it was meant to dissolve. On the consciousness map they share one panpsychism node, split from Kastrup's idealism.

Bernardo Kastrup's analytic idealism goes a step further and inverts the whole picture: mind is the only fundamental category, and what looks like a physical brain generating consciousness is actually consciousness appearing, from outside, as a physical brain — a metaphor he unpacks through dissociation (one mind appearing, from an outside view, as multiple separate things) and through altered states as evidence the brain filters or localizes experience rather than manufacturing it. That is the James filter, above, recruited as metaphysics. On the consciousness map he is now his own upper-left node, split from the Goff–Strawson panpsychism dot: experience-in-matter is not the same claim as only-mind-is-real.

Miri Albahari's perennial idealism (2019) is the Buddhist-analytic hinge on the same current: a distinction between consciousness as such and the constructed subject, drawn from Indian sources rather than from Kastrup's dissociation metaphor. She sits here so the Upanishads/Buddha boxes in the prehistory section have a contemporary argument, not just an Axial cameo. On the consciousness map she is now her own upper-left node, split from Kastrup the way Kastrup was split from Goff–Strawson.

Federico Faggin — physicist-engineer who designed the first commercial microprocessor — pushes panpsychism into a quantum-information form he calls Quantum-Information Panpsychism (QIP), developed with Giacomo Mauro D’Ariano and set out in Irreducible (2024). Consciousness is not an emergent brain product but the system's private experience of its own quantum state; classical digital machines process syntactic information with no interior, so AI can imitate intelligence without ever being aware. Wavefunction collapse, on this reading, is participatory rather than merely epistemic; matter is the outer face of an inner reality. Against Goff–Strawson panpsychism he claims entanglement solves combination (qualia states compose by quantum binding); against Penrose's Orch OR he does not wait for new microtubule physics to dissolve the hard problem — he makes experience fundamental and lets quantum physics supervene on it. On our consciousness map he sits upper-left with the other simulation-sceptics who keep the hard problem live by refusing classical computation a seat at the table.

David Bohm belongs in the same upper-left cluster, but by a different route. In Wholeness and the Implicate Order (1980) the familiar world of separate objects is only the explicate order — a relatively stable unfoldment from a deeper, undivided implicate order or holomovement. Mind and matter are both projections of that whole, not one producing the other. In the ontological (pilot-wave) interpretation he developed with Hiley, the quantum wave carries active information that informs particle motion rather than pushing it mechanically — a primitive mind-like pole already at the quantum level (what later commentators call panprotopsychism). That softens Chalmers's hard problem without Faggin's full primacy of consciousness: you no longer ask how dead matter births experience, but how a system that already has physical and informational aspects at every level yields full human awareness. Against Turing-style simulation Bohm's objection is architectural — a classical machine starts from fragments and never recovers the prior whole. Against Penrose he shares the quantum bet but refuses a local Orch-OR mechanism as the whole story; against Faggin he keeps mind and matter as co-equal unfoldments rather than making matter a mere expression of consciousness. (The Science & Philosophy Institute's own appeal to Wholeness tracks Bohm here more closely than Faggin's idealism-leaning stack.) On the consciousness map he sits with Faggin and McGilchrist.

Iain McGilchrist — psychiatrist and literary scholar, The Master and His Emissary (2009) and The Matter With Things (2021) — gives the same anti-fragmentation quarrel a different anatomy. Forget the pop-psych cartoon of “left = logic, right = creativity.” His claim is about two modes of attention that every creature needs: a narrow, targeted left-hemisphere grasp that takes the world apart into fixed, explicit, decontextualized bits (map, diagram, category, utility), and a right-hemisphere openness that holds the living whole — flowing, implicit, contextual, unique, present rather than merely represented (he leans on Heidegger's presencing here). Analysis is the emissary; the master is the grasp of the gestalt. Modern culture, on his reading, has inverted the hierarchy: too much left-hemisphere thinking, not too little — bureaucracy, reduction, certainty, confabulation when the right is offline. Science still needs the analytic donkey-work, but the big steps arrive as re-cohered wholes (the music-practice metaphor: take the piece apart to learn it, then forget the fragments and play). On mind and matter he is an explicit panpsychist: consciousness is an ontological primitive that never “emerged” from dead stuff; what we call matter is one way consciousness can show up, supplying the resistance and persistence creation needs (his ice/water/steam image). The machine metaphor — creeping since the Enlightenment, now the default — is therefore the wrong metaphor for selves: anything complex and sui generis can only be understood by metaphor, and the question is whether you have the right one; AI can excel at left-hemisphere tasks and never do the right-hemisphere leap. On the consciousness map he sits with Bohm and Faggin: simulation-sceptic, hard problem kept live by refusing to let the map replace the world.

The sharpest rejection, though, comes from inside psychoanalysis. Orthodox Lacanians in the tradition of Jacques-Alain Miller have argued explicitly against neuro-grounding projects like Solms's or Johnston's — not because the neuroscience is wrong, but because the subject Lacan describes is a logical and discursive entity, constituted entirely within the chain of signifiers, categorically different in kind from anything biology studies. On this view, looking for the unconscious in a brain scan is a category error on the order of looking for the rules of chess in the physics of the pieces: true at its own level, and answering a question nobody asked.

A rough three-tier stack

If you force the whole survey into one developmental story, it sorts into three layers — from most physically settled to most language-mediated:

chemistry

Subcortical affect circuits (Panksepp systems), hypothalamic/brainstem homeostasis, neurotransmitter tone → the id. The best-evidenced link in the whole survey.

circuitry + environment

Prefrontal maturation regulating subcortical arousal, shaped by attachment and caregiver interaction (oxytocin, mirror-neuron social learning) → the ego and early conscience. Moderately grounded.

language + society

Perisylvian language network reorganized during acquisition, mentalizing/theory-of-mind networks (medial PFC, TPJ) internalizing a social narrative → the Symbolic order, superego, ego-ideal. Least directly measurable — mostly philosophical bridging (Lacan, Žižek/Johnston, Hofstadter).

The honest shape of this: the deeper a construct sits toward affect and homeostasis, the more literally it maps — Solms would say the id basically is Panksepp's circuits, not a metaphor for them. The deeper a construct sits toward language and social positioning — ego-ideal, the big Other, objet a — the more it stays a redescription at a different level, useful for clinical and phenomenological work, not yet (maybe not ever) a wiring diagram. Churchland's caution is the right one to keep: some of this vocabulary may get replaced rather than reduced once the neuroscience matures further. And that's the optimistic version — the dissenters above would say no amount of neuroscience closes the gap at all, because the thing being explained was never physical to begin with.

A closing rumination: fiction's own register

There's a register this whole survey has left out: fiction. Every entry above got graded by how well it maps onto tissue or holds up as philosophical argument, but a lot of what these concepts have actually been used for, clinically and culturally, has never waited on either. Freud built his own case histories — Dora, the Rat Man, the Wolf Man — as narratives, and was uneasy about it: he said as much, half-apologetically, in the Studies on Hysteria, admitting they read more like short stories than clinical papers. His 1907 essay on Jensen's novella Gradiva goes further still, treating a fictional character's delusion with the same clinical seriousness as an actual patient's — on the premise that imaginative writers had already, intuitively, mapped terrain psychoanalysis was only now describing systematically.

That has to be read next to the rebuttals section above, not around it. Crews's charge is specifically that some of that narrative quality in Freud's own case histories wasn't artful compression of real clinical material — Anna O.'s outcome misreported, the Wolf Man's cure overstated — it was invention presented as fact. That's a different and worse thing than literary borrowing, and this argument doesn't get to skip past it: reading like a story is only harmless when everyone already knows it's a story.

Where fiction is doing this work openly — a novel, a myth, a short story nobody mistakes for a case report — the picture is cleaner. Peter Brooks's Reading for the Plot (1984) treats narrative form itself as running on Freudian drive dynamics, using Beyond the Pleasure Principle's account of tension, delay, and discharge as a working model for how a plot generates and manages suspense toward an ending. Nothing in that argument makes a claim about neurons, or pretends to be reporting a real patient; the payoff is that a Freudian concept illuminates how acknowledged stories work on readers, which is a different kind of confirmation than a brain scan and not obviously a weaker one. Lacan's own most famous piece of close reading, his seminar on Poe's The Purloined Letter, does something similar from the other direction: rather than applying psychoanalysis to a story, he uses the story — openly fictional throughout — to demonstrate the theory: the letter's position, changing hands but never opened or read by the reader, dictates each character's relation to it regardless of their individual psychology, which is Lacan's whole claim about the signifying chain's priority over the subject, made vivid in a way the bare formula isn't. Date that reading: it is middle-early Symbolic Lacan, the letter as pure signifier. The late sinthome via Joyce is a different machine, argued on Fiction of the maps.

None of this needs a physical referent to do its work, and none of it rescues the clinical claims either — whether psychoanalytic therapy actually outperforms no treatment, Eysenck's question above, is a separate matter this register doesn't touch one way or the other. What survives is narrower, and probably more honest: a wiring diagram, however complete, was never going to answer why wanting feels like anything, and neither was a patient's recovery rate — but a story everyone agrees is a story can still gesture at what that's like from the inside, not by evidence or argument but by something closer to recognition. That's a third register alongside the empirical and the philosophical the argument has been sorting by: not measured, not argued, just staged, on the condition that it's staged in the open. It may be the actual reason these categories have stayed useful in English departments and in how people narrate their own lives, whatever the state of the couch itself.

Prehistory & modern setup

Avidya
In the early Upanishads, not-knowing what the self actually is — craving and misrecognition binding a person to suffering. Not Freud's dynamic unconscious, but an Axial claim that the conscious agent is not the last word on what moves it. Where this is used ↑
Akrasia
Acting against one's own better judgment — Plato and Aristotle's moral-psychology hinge for opaque motive before a Freudian repression model exists. Where this is used ↑
Cogito / mind–body dualism
Descartes's certainty that thinking cannot doubt itself away, paired with mind as thinking substance and body as extended matter — the modern split later names keep rediscovering. Where this is used ↑
Petites perceptions
Leibniz's minute perceptions below awareness that nonetheless shape conscious experience — an early clean claim that unconscious mental activity does real work continuously. Where this is used ↑
Will (Schopenhauer)
Blind, non-rational striving underlying behavior, with intellect a secondary often self-deceived servant — the most-cited philosophical ancestor of the Freudian id. Where this is used ↑

Freud

Topographical model
Freud's 1900 split of mind by accessibility: unconscious, preconscious, conscious. Where this is used ↑
Structural model
Freud's 1923 split by function: id, ego, and superego (with ego-ideal as the positive pole of the latter). Where this is used ↑
Id
Inherited reservoir of drives running on primary process — condensation, displacement, no contradiction, no time. Solms/Panksepp later cash this out as subcortical affect systems, especially SEEKING. Where this is used ↑
Ego
What forms at the interface with the external world — reality-testing and delayed gratification — later read through bodily/interoceptive maps (Damasio) and prefrontal regulation. Where this is used ↑
Superego / ego-ideal
Internalized parental/social prohibition (superego) and its positive pole aimed at rather than forbidding (ego-ideal) — the image of who one ought to become. Where this is used ↑
Primary / secondary process
Primary: drive logic without contradiction or time; secondary: reality-oriented, delayed, discursive. Edelman's primary vs higher-order consciousness is a late neural rhyme, not an identity. Where this is used ↑
Repression
Pushing contents out of accessibility so they return in symptoms, slips, and dreams — Herbart's threshold competition is a conceptual ancestor; Grünbaum and Crews contest the clinical evidence. Where this is used ↑

Jung

Collective unconscious
Jung's layer of psychic life shared across individuals, not reducible to personal repressed biography — the conceptual break from Freud's archival/sexual unconscious. Where this is used ↑
Archetype
Inherited organizing form (Mother, Shadow, Self, …) that appears in dreams, myth, and symptom as a type-variation rather than as a disguised personal memory. Where this is used ↑
Individuation
Jung's clinical aim: becoming a more differentiated whole by integrating what the ego has cast out — including the shadow — rather than primarily lifting repression of infantile wish. Where this is used ↑

Klein

Paranoid-schizoid / depressive positions
Klein's two modes of relating to internal objects, not ladder-stages: splitting the object to manage annihilating anxiety (paranoid-schizoid), then integrating good and bad in one object with guilt and reparation (depressive). Where this is used ↑
Projective identification
Klein's 1946 mechanism: unwanted parts of the self are forced into the other, who may then be lived as containing them — a two-person unconscious, not a one-person archive of wishes. Where this is used ↑
Unconscious phantasy
The British school's spelling-with-ph: mental expression of drive from the start, not a later decoration of need. Susan Isaacs's 1943 Controversial Discussions paper is the Kleinian line. Distinct from conscious fantasy and from Panksepp's PLAY circuit. Where this is used ↑
Envy (Klein)
Attack on the good object because it is good — the breast that feeds hated for having what the infant does not. Not greed, not Kojève's recognition, not Girard's copied wanting. Gratitude is the depressive-position capacity that can receive the good without spoiling it. Pin: Envy and Gratitude (1957). Where this is used ↑
Reparation
The depressive position's wish to repair the damaged whole object — Klein's ethics of concern after splitting. Not a moral add-on; it is what integration costs. Where this is used ↑
Symbol formation (Segal)
Hanna Segal (1957): symbolic equation collapses symbol into thing (psychotic concrete); a true symbol requires the depressive position's tolerance of absence. The Kleinian path from part-object to language; meets Lacan's Symbolic without becoming it. Where this is used ↑

Lacan & after

Real / Symbolic / Imaginary (RSI)
Lacan's three registers, mapped by McGowan onto Kant's sensibility / understanding / reason: Imaginary (what you see: image, ego, rivalry), Symbolic (the position from which you see: law, language, Name-of-the-Father), Real (what reason-like leftover resists — early, what returns to the same place, outside; middle, internal torsion of the Symbolic). Timeline pin: 1953 Rome discourse / RSI turn. Where this is used ↑
Mirror stage
Infant identifying with a unified image ahead of motor mastery — founding misrecognition of the ego (canonical text 1949). Short enough to anthologize, which is why it got made to stand for all of Lacan; not original to him, not the system's load-bearing invention. The developmental lag it cites is real. Where this is used ↑
Objet a
Object-cause of desire, invented in Seminar IX (1961–62): not the object of desire but the obstacle that makes it desirable — remainder, waste, the crinkle that keeps a perfect object seeming to exist. Klein's part-object is the clinical leftover it philosophizes, not the same concept. Any "small a" before that seminar is not this concept. Lacan later called it what he invented. Where this is used ↑
Quilting point (point de capiton)
Seminar III (1956): a privileged signifier that stops the slide of meaning and retroactively decides what came before — like a period, or the button that keeps quilt stuffing from drifting. Lacan drops it after ~1960. Not the same job as the master signifier, though most readers treat them as one word. Where this is used ↑
Master signifier (S1)
A signifier without a signified that starts a chain and orients a subject ("because I said so") without itself meaning anything. Later name for the Name-of-the-Father. Grounds; does not punctuate. McGowan (2025): theory quilts a situation, it does not install a new S1. Where this is used ↑
Sinthome
Late Lacan (Seminar XXIII, via Joyce): a privileged symptom that cannot be interpreted away and instead knots a singular subject together once a shared master signifier no longer holds the chain. Does not coexist, in Lacan's own run, with the early quilting point. Where this is used ↑
Formulas of sexuation
Seminar XX's logical writing of masculine and feminine as a non-relation ("there is no sexual relation"). Copjec maps them onto Kant's antinomies; Zupančič reads political ontology from the feminine non-all; McGowan treats the same stretch as a retreat from Hegelian contradiction. Where this is used ↑
Semiotic (Kristeva)
Rhythmic, bodily, drive-charged organization under the Symbolic — pre-verbal sound and maternal relation that keeps erupting through language as poetic excess. Kleinian weather: the 2000 Melanie Klein book treats the mother as source of thought, not only of feeding. Where this is used ↑
Weak nature (Johnston)
Johnston's claim that nature is gappy and self-inconsistent rather than a seamless deterministic block — so a free subject can arise inside materialism without supernatural surplus. Where this is used ↑
Destructive plasticity (Malabou)
Plasticity not only as adaptive reshaping but as explosive loss of form (trauma, lesion) — necessary for Johnston, not sufficient alone for a Hegelian-Lacanian subject. Where this is used ↑
Event / subject (Badiou)
Badiou's subject as fidelity to an event that cannot be deduced from the prior situation — the foil that clarifies what Johnston's biological underdetermination is not doing. Where this is used ↑

Neuroscience hinges

SEEKING
Panksepp's dopaminergic appetitive/exploratory affect system — Solms's closest empirical cashing-out of Freudian drive/wanting and of dreaming's forebrain permission. Where this is used ↑
Activation-synthesis
Hobson & McCarley's claim that dreams are the forebrain's narrative synthesis of essentially meaningless pontine activation in REM — the main foil to Freudian wish-dreaming. Where this is used ↑
Continuity hypothesis / DMN
Domhoff's view that dream content mostly continues waking concerns and correlates with default-mode network activity — dreaming as self-modeling side effect more than royal road. Describes content; does not settle why the brain bothers. Where this is used ↑
Threat / social simulation
Revonsuo (2000): dreams rehearse threat perception and avoidance as an evolved offline function. Later, with Valli and Tuominen, social simulation — interpersonal scenes as practice, not a disguised Wunsch. Organized dreaming that refuses both Hobson's noise and Freud's dream-work. Where this is used ↑
NEXTUP
Zadra and Stickgold (2021): Network Exploration to Understand Possibilities — REM explores weak associations across memory so knowledge can generalize. The dream is the experience of that search, not a coded wish. Where this is used ↑
Reentry / TNGS
Edelman's Theory of Neuronal Group Selection: experience selects among overproduced neuronal groups; reentry binds maps into a coherent scene without a central homunculus. Where this is used ↑
Core / autobiographical self
Damasio's split between moment-to-moment homeostatic feeling (core) and narrative self built from memory — a cashing-out of Freud's bodily-ego footnote. Where this is used ↑
Predictive processing / precision
Friston-style hierarchical inference: the brain predicts sensory input and weights prediction errors by precision — one mechanistic bridge proposed for repression and the Real. Seth's beast-machine and Barrett's constructed emotion are the same architecture from the inside of a living body. Where this is used ↑
Enactive / neurophenomenology
Consciousness as sense-making by a living, world-coupled body (Varela, Thompson, Rosch), not an inner theatre a program could copy. Neurophenomenology is Varela's later demand that first-person method meet third-person data. Where this is used ↑

Rebuttals & dissent

Falsifiability
Popper's criterion (pinned here to Conjectures and Refutations, 1963; already in the 1934/1959 Logic): a theory that can accommodate every outcome equally well explains none — his charge against psychoanalysis. Where this is used ↑
Tally argument
Freud's clinical claim that symptom relief after accepting an interpretation vindicates the interpretation — Grünbaum's target: it cannot separate correctness from suggestion. Where this is used ↑
Hard problem
Chalmers's distinction: easy functional problems of mind vs why any functioning is accompanied by subjective experience at all. Where this is used ↑
Mary's Room
Jackson's knowledge argument (1982): Mary knows all physical facts about color yet learns something on seeing red — evidence against physicalism (Jackson later recanted the anti-physicalist reading). Where this is used ↑
Panpsychism / analytic idealism
Experience as a basic feature of matter (panpsychism) or mind as the only fundamental category (Kastrup's analytic idealism; Albahari's perennial idealism from Indian sources) — dissent from reductive neural grounding. Faggin's Quantum-Information Panpsychism is the quantum-theoretic cousin: consciousness as the private experience of a pure quantum state. Bohm's implicate order / active information is closer to dual-aspect monism or panprotopsychism: mind and matter co-unfold from undivided wholeness. McGilchrist's version makes consciousness an ontological primitive and treats matter as one manifestation of that field (resistance + persistence), reached via hemisphere modes of attention rather than quantum formalism. Where this is used ↑
Implicate / explicate order
Bohm's distinction: the familiar world of separate objects (explicate) as a relatively stable unfoldment from a deeper undivided holomovement (implicate). Where this is used ↑
Master / emissary (hemisphere attention)
McGilchrist's pair: right-hemisphere openness to a living, contextual whole (master) versus left-hemisphere narrow grasp that fragments the world into useful bits (emissary). The cultural pathology is when the map/emissary usurps the master. Where this is used ↑
