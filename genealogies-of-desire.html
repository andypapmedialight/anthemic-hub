Genealogies of Desire

Freud and Marx were fused in the 1920s, blown apart by French theory in the 1970s, remixed into cyberpunk philosophy at a UK university philosophy department in the 1990s, and split again — one branch communist, one branch neoreactionary. The other lineage this project's Freud could have gone down.

NOTE — this branch is intellectual history and political theory, not an empirical grounding project like the neuroscience piece it forks from. "How well-evidenced" isn't really the right axis here; "how influential" and "what it was reacting against" are. Read the margin notes accordingly — they track reception and controversy, not experimental support.

Kant and Hegel 1780s–1830s, the deep root

Everything in this document is downstream of a fight that starts here, well before the twentieth-century rooms that later redistributed it. Immanuel Kant's Critique of Pure Reason (1781) is built around a claim that looked, to his predecessors, like a contradiction in terms: the synthetic a priori. To see why, Kant crosses two distinctions. A priori judgments are known independently of any particular sensory episode — they claim strict universality and necessity (it must be so in every possible case). A posteriori judgments arise from experience — "the sun is warm," this crow is black — and so give particular instances, not that things must be that way everywhere. Kant's own clarification matters: all knowledge begins with experience in a causal sense (you would never have arithmetic without having learned to count), but a judgment can still be a priori if experience cannot justify it. Once you have the concepts, "7 + 5 = 12" is not confirmed by looking harder at objects; nothing you see could overturn it the way a warm morning could overturn "the sun is always cold."

Cross that with a second cut. Analytic judgments are explicative: the predicate is already contained in the subject-concept — "all bodies are extended," or "all bachelors are unmarried." Analyzing the concept clarifies it; it adds no new information. Synthetic judgments are ampliative: the predicate is not packed into the subject — "all bodies have weight," or "all the crows I have seen are black." That crossing yields four boxes, two of which Kant treats as empty or uninteresting. Analytic a posteriori drops out: if the predicate is already in the concept, you do not need experience to extract it. Analytic a priori is real but thin — pure clarification of concepts. Synthetic a posteriori is ordinary empirical knowledge: weight added to body, blackness added to crow. The load-bearing claim of the first Critique is the remaining box. There are synthetic a priori judgments — known through pure reason, independent of justifying experience, yet genuinely ampliative. Empiricists after Hume had treated ampliative knowledge as always contingent on the senses; rationalists after Leibniz had treated necessary, universal knowledge as always analytic. Kant's wager is that mathematics, the basic laws of natural science, and the legitimate core of metaphysics sit in that third kind. "7 + 5 = 12" is not analytic: you can turn over the concepts of seven, five, and addition as long as you like without finding twelve already inside them; the sum has to be constructed. "A triangle has three sides" is analytic a priori; "the interior angles of a triangle sum to 180°" is, for Kant, synthetic a priori — universal and necessary, yet not readable off the mere definition of triangle; you need construction (Euclid's style of proof, applied to the essential properties of any triangle, not to this crooked chalk drawing). "Every event has a cause" is the same kind of claim for physics. Without judgments like these, Kant argues, you would have either empty logic or a heap of contingent observations, and no bridge to objective knowledge of a shared world.

How such judgments are possible is what transcendental idealism is for. Human experience, on Kant's account, does not mirror reality "as it is in itself"; it is structured in advance by innate frameworks of the mind. The forms of intuition — space and time — are a priori structures of sensibility, not properties we read off things; they are what make geometry and arithmetic possible as synthetic a priori sciences in the first place. The categories of the understanding — causality, substance, and the rest of Kant's twelve — are the concepts through which the mind synthesizes the manifold of sensory data into coherent, law-governed experience, which is why something like "every event has a cause" can be known as necessary for any possible experience of objects, not merely induced from habit as Hume had concluded. The cost of that solution is the famous split this whole lineage keeps returning to: phenomena (things as those forms and categories necessarily structure them) versus noumena (things as they are in themselves, permanently unreachable by that structuring).

Buried in the same book is the quieter move that ties this page to the sibling neuroscience essay's Descartes. The "I think" that must be able to accompany all of a person's representations — the transcendental unity of apperception — is a formal precondition for having unified experience at all, not a persisting soul-substance. Descartes's Meditations had treated the cogito as revealing a thinking thing (res cogitans), a mind-substance really distinct from body; the Paralogisms chapter of the first Critique argues that inference is illegitimate. From "I think" you get a thinking act that can accompany representations; you do not get metaphysical knowledge of a simple, immortal, substantial soul. Rational psychology, on Kant's diagnosis, confuses a formal condition of experience with an object known in itself. That is the bridge this whole document keeps crossing under different names: a subject as something a system does, not something a system has — the same crack Lacan and Hofstadter reopen on the grounding page, and the same Cartesian knowing subject Heidegger will later displace with Dasein, below.

A Scottish side-branch answers the same early-modern corridor from the other direction. Thomas Reid's Inquiry into the Human Mind on the Principles of Common Sense (1764) refuses the way of ideas that runs from Descartes through Locke's Essay into Berkeley and Hume: the claim that what we immediately know are mental representations, with the external world (or other minds) always an inference away. That setup, Reid argues, makes radical skepticism look mandatory once you take it seriously — so he rejects the setup. Common sense, for him, names first principles of belief (that there is an external world, that memory is generally trustworthy, that other minds exist) that philosophy has no standing to dissolve without using them. Hume is the skeptic who woke Kant; Reid is the other exit from the same corridor — restore trust in perception and judgment rather than rebuild objectivity through transcendental forms. This page follows Kant into Hegel and desire, not Reid into Scottish common-sense realism; the pin is here so the epistemology fork is marked. Locke the political theorist of property and personhood sits on the history page beside Hobbes and Mills.

Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit (1807) treats Kant's phenomena/noumena wall as an artifact of not yet being dialectical enough, and replaces the static split with a developmental one: consciousness passes through and overcomes its own successive, self-undermining shapes — sense-certainty, perception, understanding, self-consciousness, reason — on the way to a fuller self-knowledge. Its most consequential single passage for everything that follows is lordship and bondage (the master-slave dialectic): self-consciousness only becomes genuinely self-conscious through a life-and-death struggle for recognition with another self-consciousness, producing an unequal master/slave relation that then dialectically inverts, since the slave's formative labor on the object gives the slave a more genuine self-relation than the master ever gets from simply being obeyed. This is the actual text Kojève will read, a century later, four chapters into a much longer book, as fundamentally a theory of desire.

A separate, stranger Hegel passage — the 1805–06 Jena manuscripts' "night of the world," consciousness as an abyss where disconnected images surface and vanish — becomes, over a century later, Žižek's source for the self-relating negativity covered in the neuroscience piece's Žižek section, and Adrian Johnston's "transcendental materialism" there borrows its name directly from the Kantian sense above: conditions of possibility, not something beyond nature.

how this landed

Synthetic a priori — still the load-bearing claim of the first Critique; later philosophy of mathematics and science has argued hard over whether Euclidean geometry and causal law really work the way Kant needs them to (non-Euclidean geometries and revised physics put pressure on the triangle and causality examples especially), but the problem he named — necessity that isn't mere definition — did not go away.

Kant's Paralogisms — still the standard reference point for "the subject as function, not substance" arguments; the direct target is Cartesian rational psychology as rebuilt after the Meditations. Both Lacan's and Hofstadter's versions of that claim, in the sibling piece, sit in this lineage whether or not either cites Kant directly. The bridge runs both ways: Grounding needs Descartes for the mind–body problem; this page needs Kant's refusal of the soul-substance for everything after that treats the subject as a formal or linguistic function. Lacan also imports Kant more quietly into the registers themselves: Imaginary ≈ sensibility, Symbolic ≈ understanding, Real ≈ reason that overruns what it can know — a mapping Grounding now dates across three seminar periods rather than treating RSI as one frozen gadget.

Reid — Scottish common-sense realism remains a live minority option in epistemology and philosophy of perception; this page marks the fork and does not follow it. Kant is the exit this genealogy actually takes from the Hume problem.

Lordship and bondage — a small fraction of a very long book that became, largely through Kojève, wildly disproportionate to its own space in the Phenomenology; most Hegel scholars read it as one moment in a longer argument, not the book's real point.

"Night of the world" — a pre-Phenomenology text Hegel never published this way; Žižek's heavy reliance on it is itself contested by some Hegel scholars as reading a mature system back into discarded early drafts.

Nietzsche 1872–1888, genealogy as a method

genealogy as methodmaster / slave moralityressentimentwill to power

This whole document's method has a name, and Nietzsche invented it. On the Genealogy of Morals (1887) doesn't ask what "good" and "evil" really are, in some timeless sense; it asks how those words came to mean what they mean — out of which historical relations of force, serving which interests, at whose expense. That's genealogy in Nietzsche's specific sense: not an origin story that explains a thing by its essence, but a history of descent that explains a thing by the accidents, reversals, and power struggles that produced it, with no guarantee the story resolves anywhere in particular. It's the deliberate opposite of Hegel's dialectic, above: no necessary movement toward a fuller self-knowledge, no reconciliation waiting at the end — just one contingent reversal after another.

The book's central case study is a genealogy of morality itself. Master morality praises what the strong naturally are — vital, assertive, unapologetic — and calls it "good," with "bad" a mere afterthought (whatever isn't that). Slave morality inverts the whole scale: unable to act on their own strength, the powerless perform a revaluation instead, declaring the master's vitality itself evil and their own patience and self-denial "good." Nietzsche's name for the psychological fuel behind that inversion is ressentiment — a reactive, self-poisoning resentment that can't discharge itself in action, so it discharges in judgment instead. It's a strikingly different master/slave story than Hegel's: Hegel's slave gains a more genuine self-relation through labor; Nietzsche's slave wins by a hostile trick of valuation, and Nietzsche thinks the trick, not the labor, is what actually built modern morality.

The Genealogy's second essay gets closer to psychoanalysis than anything else in nineteenth-century philosophy: bad conscience, Nietzsche argues, is what happens when cruelty and aggression that used to discharge outward — in raids, punishment, open violence — get walled in by society and turned back against their own owner. That self-directed cruelty is what conscience, guilt, and the sense of having an "inner depth" actually are: not achievements of civilization, but civilization's violence redirected inward. It's the direct textual ancestor of Freud's superego a few decades later, covered in the neuroscience piece's prehistory section — including Freud's own documented, anxious avoidance of reading Nietzsche closely. Jung, by contrast, claimed the Nietzschean package outright (Zarathustra seminars, inflation, the thin rational ego) — the other depth-psychology reception of the same source.

Underneath both moral genealogies sits Nietzsche's will to power: the proposal that every drive, institution, and even every act of knowing is best read as a form of power seeking to expand and overcome resistance, not a neutral fact waiting to be discovered. Two very different later readers pick this up and run in opposite directions. Deleuze's Nietzsche and Philosophy, below, turns it into affirmative difference — force and intensity as positive and productive, irreducible to negation, the direct philosophical weapon against Hegel that Deleuze and Guattari later fire politically. Land's Warwick-era writing, much further below, turns the same vocabulary into something closer to its opposite of Nietzsche's own individualist ideal: an impersonal, machinic force — capital itself — that uses human institutions as a vehicle and answers to no one's self-overcoming but its own.

One more debt is close to a direct citation. Foucault's own essay "Nietzsche, Genealogy, History" (1971) states outright that the method running through his own later work, below, is Nietzschean genealogy applied to modern institutions: no hidden essence of madness, sexuality, or the prison waiting to be uncovered, only a history of the accidents and power struggles that produced the categories in the first place. Foucault's repressive hypothesis, below, is Nietzschean genealogy pointed directly at Reich and Marcuse's account of sexual repression, above.

how this landed

Genealogy as method — now a standard historical and critical technique far beyond Nietzsche's own moral psychology; Foucault's version is the most influential contemporary descendant.

Ressentiment — widely used across critical theory and political commentary, sometimes loosely; Nietzsche's own use is more psychologically specific than its popularized descendants.

Will to power — badly distorted by his sister Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche's posthumous editing (The Will to Power, 1901/1906) into something aligned with nationalist and antisemitic politics Nietzsche explicitly rejected in his own writing; most serious scholarship treats that volume as a compromised, editorially constructed text, not his book.

Land's repurposing — a modern, deliberately impersonal reading of will-to-power vocabulary, not evidence of Nietzsche's own politics; worth keeping those two things separate given how often his name gets attached to whatever borrows his terms. Fisher later called Land "our Nietzsche"; Noys, in the 2025 preface, treats that lineage as the unpaid irrationalist debt compromising both right and left accelerationism.

The Line Between — Nietzsche's genealogical reading of the quarrel is restaged in the play.

Kojève's Hegel 1933–1939, the seminar that transmitted him

master-slave dialecticdesire for recognitionthe seminar everyone attended

A century after Hegel, the text above reaches twentieth-century French thought largely through one room in Paris. Alexandre Kojève — Russian-born, eventually a French civil servant, an idiosyncratic reader of Hegel who folded in Marx and Heidegger along the way — ran a seminar on Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit at the École Pratique des Hautes Études from 1933 to 1939 that turned out to be one of the highest-leverage rooms in twentieth-century French thought. The attendance list alone explains a lot of what follows: Jacques Lacan, Georges Bataille, Maurice Merleau-Ponty, Raymond Aron, and Raymond Queneau, who later edited the seminar transcripts into the book most people actually read. Lacan was not a casual auditor. He called Kojève his only master and arranged a second weekly session with a couple of others at his house — dinner for teaching, double the Hegel. Kojève's central move was reading Hegel's master-slave dialectic as a theory of desire itself: desire isn't for objects, it's for recognition by another desiring consciousness, which makes human desire structurally social and reciprocal from the outset, driving a historical dialectic Kojève argued had, in his own reading, effectively reached its end. That is also how "desire" gets into Freud: the German Wunsch is wish; Lacan, after this room, hears unconscious desire whenever Freud talks about the unconscious.

The Heidegger Kojève folded in was not a side interest. Martin Heidegger's Being and Time (1927) had already displaced the Cartesian knowing subject with Dasein — being-there, thrown into a world of practical concern rather than standing over objects as a detached cogito — and had made authenticity, care, and being-toward-death the vocabulary for what a finite existence could own. Kojève reads Hegel's struggle for recognition through that existential pressure: the master-slave fight is not just a social puzzle about status, but a life-and-death confrontation in which desire risks itself. This piece does not follow Heidegger into fundamental ontology, the later "turn," or the political catastrophe of his Nazism — but the question of technology, Gestell, and what Stiegler did with that later work now has its own map in Technics and Time. The Kojève–Sartre–Lacan corridor below is hard to hear without that 1927 break from Descartes still ringing in the room.

Simone de Beauvoir takes the same Hegelian recognition structure and asks what happens when one of the two self-consciousnesses is socially cast as Other rather than peer. The Second Sex (1949) is not a Kojève seminar transcript, but it is the most consequential mid-century rereading of lordship-and-bondage as a sexed situation: woman is made the inessential opposite against which man claims the subject-position of the essential, and "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman" names that becoming as historical work, not biology. Beauvoir writes inside the Sartre–existentialist room that also absorbed Heidegger and Hegel; this genealogy keeps her here because desire-for-recognition without her is still a story told as if the recognizers were unmarked equals. Du Bois and Fanon, on the history page, press the same recognition dialectic under racialization; Mahmood, below, asks what happens when the feminist subject who would "become" is already a pious one.

Lacan is the direct line into the psychoanalytic material covered in the neuroscience piece this one branches from: his claim that desire is always the desire of the Other is Kojève's recognition-structure translated into psychoanalytic terms. That is the early-to-middle formula. From Seminar IX the same career qualifies Kojève hard: objet a is not recognition by another consciousness but the obstacle that makes an object desirable — a Hegelian torsion inside the Symbolic, not a struggle between two self-consciousnesses. Date the claim before you mix those two Lacans. Klein's envy is a different machine again, and not a Paris one: wanting that spoils the source because the source is good — not Kojève's recognition, not Girard's copied wanting, not desiring-production. Deleuze and Guattari went the opposite direction — Anti-Oedipus's whole desiring-production apparatus, below, is built explicitly against this Hegelian picture of desire as lack chasing recognition, insisting desire is positive and productive from the start, not a structural absence waiting to be filled. Merleau-Ponty took a third route entirely, into phenomenology proper, a tributary this piece doesn't follow further — the enactive, embodied position that lineage licenses now sits on the consciousness map (Varela, Thompson, Rosch), not as a fifth desire-school. A fourth corridor answers Kojève from fieldwork and the novel rather than from that Paris room: Mead, Bateson, and Girard, next.

Georges Bataille took the seminar somewhere stranger still. Where Kojève's closed, fully satisfied post-historical subject has nowhere left to go, Bataille's economy of expenditure, transgression, and sovereignty — worked out across the Collège de Sociologie years and later in The Accursed Share (1949) — hunts for whatever utility, recognition, and the end of history cannot contain: sacrifice, erotic excess, the sacred as waste rather than production. That refusal of Kojève's terminus runs downstream into Baudrillard's symbolic exchange and the Situationist critique of spectacle, below, and it is the explicit early academic route Nick Land takes into Kant and annihilation in The Thirst for Annihilation (1992). The genealogies diagram's "via Bataille" edge is not a flourish — it is the hinge this section is naming.

how this landed

Kojève — historians of philosophy generally treat this reading as more Kojève than Hegel: brilliant and hugely generative, but a real departure from the text, not a neutral commentary.

Heidegger — folded into Kojève's Hegel as existential pressure on recognition; Gestell, Simondon, and Stiegler are on Technics and Time.

Beauvoir — still the default mid-century feminist rereading of Hegelian Otherness; later difference feminism and Butler, below, both argue with and against her.

Bataille — the expenditure/sovereignty refusal of the "end of history" is the hinge into Baudrillard, the Situationists, and Land's early Bataille book; not a full Bataille monograph.

Attendance list — well documented, since the seminar was recorded and later edited by Queneau; how directly each attendee was actually shaped by it, versus absorbing it secondhand, is debated case by case. Lacan's extra home sessions and "only master" line are the strongest single-person case.

"End of history" — Kojève's own version predates, and differs substantially from, Francis Fukuyama's much later and more famous use of the same phrase; worth not conflating the two.

The Line Between — this quarrel is restaged in the play, in Kojève's voice.

Mead · Bateson · Girard — the next section is the anthropological-cybernetic answer to recognition-desire, not a footnote on the seminar attendance list.

Klein — envy as a desire-machine that is not recognition sits on the grounding page; named here so Kojève is not the only theory of wanting this genealogy admits before Paris splits.

Mead, Bateson, Girard, Taussig 1928–1993, culture, schismogenesis, mimesis

Kojève's wager, above, is that human desire is desire for recognition. This corridor keeps the claim that desire is social from the start, then relocates the mechanism. Margaret Mead and Gregory Bateson — Boasian fieldworkers, collaborators, and for a time married — treat wanting, temperament, and conflict as patterned interaction inside a culture, not as a universal struggle for the Other's acknowledgment. René Girard, a generation later and from literature rather than the village, sits among them because he offers a third social theory of desire: not recognition, not cultural personality, but imitation of a model's wanting, which then escalates into rivalry and is discharged onto a victim. He is not their student. He is the later, more universalizing anthropologist of the sacred who has to be read against the relativist generation they belong to, and against the same Hegelian-Lacanian picture of desire-as-lack this page already tracks. Michael Taussig, later still, keeps the word mimesis and refuses Girard's sacrificial universal: copying as colonial contact and sensory magic, routed through Benjamin's mimetic faculty, not through a founding murder that every culture secretly shares.

Margaret Mead's Coming of Age in Samoa (1928) is the public pin of American culture and personality: adolescence is not a biological storm that every society must weather the same way; Samoan girls, on her account, passed through it with less guilt and less crisis than the American teenagers she was writing against. Sex and Temperament in Three Primitive Societies (1935) is the load-bearing book for this genealogy. Among the Arapesh, Mundugumor, and Tchambuli (Chambri) of New Guinea, she argued that what Euro-American common sense calls masculine and feminine temperament is culturally assigned — cooperative Arapesh men and women, violent Mundugumor men and women, Tchambuli women as the managerial sex and men as the ornamented one. Desire and gender here are not a Hegelian Otherness waiting to be recognized, nor Freud's universal Oedipus; they are local pattern. Ruth Benedict's Patterns of Culture (1934) is the companion formulation — a culture as personality writ large — and the Boasian room Mead trained in; the timeline keeps her as that hinge, not as a fourth desire-school. Malinowski, on the neuroscience page, is the other anthropological cut on Freud's family romance from Trobriand kinship; Mead is the cut that travels into gender. Read toward Beauvoir, above: "one is not born, but rather becomes" has an American fieldwork rhyme a decade and a half earlier, without Beauvoir's Hegelian machinery. Read toward Spillers, later: a temperament-pattern argument still needs a body that colonial capture did not ungender. The map pin is the 1928–35 pair, not a tour of Mead's later public-intellectual career or her wartime work with US government agencies.

Gregory Bateson's Naven (1936), on the Iatmul of the Sepik, names schismogenesis: a runaway, self-reinforcing differentiation of behavior between persons or groups. Symmetrical schismogenesis is rivalry that matches and escalates (boast for boast, threat for threat). Complementary schismogenesis is a split that deepens by fitting together (dominance calling out submission, which calls out more dominance). The point is cybernetic before Bateson had the later vocabulary: conflict is a feedback loop in a relationship, not a property of a soul. The Bali fieldwork with Mead, published as Balinese Character (1942), is the other half of that loop — a culture they read as refusing climax, holding interaction on a plateau of intensity instead of letting it peak and collapse. Bateson later reused "plateau" in Steps to an Ecology of Mind (1972); Deleuze and Guattari lift the word, and the idea, into A Thousand Plateaus (1980), below — one of the few direct American-anthropology imports into that French-turn vocabulary. The 1956 Palo Alto paper "Toward a Theory of Schizophrenia," with Don Jackson, Jay Haley, and John Weakland, names the double bind: a person caught in a relationship that imposes contradictory injunctions and forbids exit or comment. Schizophrenia, on that wager, is an interactional predicament, not only an intra-psychic disease — a claim that rhymes with Guattari's later clinical politics more than with Freud's Oedipal interior, and that clinical psychiatry did not adopt as a causal theory of schizophrenia. Bateson also sat in the Macy cybernetics conferences with Mead; "information is a difference that makes a difference" is his compact, and Steps is the book that gathers the ethnographic, psychiatric, and ecological pieces as one ecology of mind.

René Girard arrives from a different desk. Deceit, Desire and the Novel (1961; French Mensonge romantique et vérité romanesque) reads Cervantes, Stendhal, Flaubert, Dostoevsky, and Proust as a single discovery: mimetic desire. We do not want objects because of their properties, or because a lack in us needs filling, or because we need the Other's recognition as Kojève has it. We want because a mediator — a model — wants, or seems to want, and our desire copies theirs. When the model is close enough to be a rival, imitation becomes conflict: two desires converging on one object. That is Girard's rewrite of both Hegelian recognition and Freudian Oedipus. The Oedipal triangle, on his account, is a mimetic rivalry misread as innate incestuous wanting; Malinowski's kinship objection and Mead's cultural-temperament objection sit beside that rewrite, not underneath it. Violence and the Sacred (1972) — the same year as Anti-Oedipus — scales the rivalry up to culture as such. Imitation that cannot be discharged produces a sacrificial crisis: undifferentiation, reciprocal violence, the social body coming apart. The resolution Girard names is the scapegoat mechanism: the crowd converges on a victim whose death or expulsion restores difference and founds the sacred, prohibition, and ritual as the memory of that murder, misrecognized as divine. Lévi-Strauss's structural anthropology, already imported into Lacan's Symbolic on the grounding page, is a named foil: Girard wants a generative mechanism (mimesis → crisis → victim) rather than a kinship combinatory. Bataille, above, hunts sacrifice as expenditure and sovereignty; Girard reads the same sacred as a cover story for founding violence, not a remainder Kojève's end of history cannot contain. Things Hidden Since the Foundation of the World (1978) and The Scapegoat (1982) make the late, explicitly Christian claim: the Gospels unveil the scapegoat mechanism instead of mythologizing it. This page keeps the desire-and-violence anthropology; it does not follow Girard into apologetics. Read against Deleuze and Guattari, below: 1972 produces two rival social theories of desire in the same season — production that decodes, versus mimesis that persecutes. Read against Federici: the witch-hunt as scapegoat drama is Girard's kind of story; primitive accumulation of women's unpaid reproductive labor is hers. They can name the same corpses and still not be the same theory. Read against Spillers: a universal mimetic crisis still presupposes subjects who can occupy the model-rival-victim grammar; New World flesh is the cut that grammar does not get for free.

Michael Taussig is why this corridor does not stop at Girard. Mimesis and Alterity: A Particular History of the Senses (1993) takes the same English root — mimesis — and rebuilds it from Benjamin's short essay on the mimetic faculty (the human capacity to become similar, to copy, to sense likeness) plus ethnography of colonial encounter in Latin America, especially Cuna figurines and other contact-zones where the colonized copy the colonizer's body, tools, and power. Mimesis here is not desire-for-a-model that escalates into scapegoating; it is a two-way, sensory, often magical practice of becoming-other under unequal conditions — alterity as the point of the copy, not the crisis the copy produces. Taussig's earlier The Devil and Commodity Fetishism in South America (1980) already sat closer to Marx's fetish than to Girard's sacred: plantation and mine workers' devil contracts as a local reading of commodity relations, not as a disguised founding murder. The fit on this page is precise and limited: Taussig is the late anthropological pressure that keeps "mimesis" from meaning only Girard's machine, and the bridge that pulls Benjamin's mimetic faculty onto a desire map without turning Benjamin into a Girardian. Read toward Said and Mahmood: the laboratory is never only the West's other. Read toward Spillers, above: copying under colonial power still meets flesh that the grammar of resemblance does not freely grant.

What holds the corridor together, and what keeps Girard from collapsing into either Parisian desire-school, is the interactional pattern — then the quarrel over whether that pattern is universal. Mead (with Benedict) says the pattern is cultural and variable. Bateson says the pattern is a feedback loop that can run to schism or be held on a plateau. Girard says the pattern is imitation, and that when it runs away the cultural solution has been sacrificial. Taussig says imitation is real and historically particular, often colonial, and does not require Girard's victim-theology to do work. Bateson's symmetrical schismogenesis and Girard's mimetic rivalry describe almost the same escalation; Bateson stays ecological and refuses a founding-murder theology, Girard names a victim and a sacred. Mead's relativism is the Boasian brake on Girard's universals — the same brake Malinowski already applied to Freud. Taussig is the second brake, from inside mimesis studies, via Benjamin. None of them is Kojève's struggle for recognition; Girard is the one in live, if mostly one-way, argument with the French theory this page turns to next; Taussig is the reason that argument does not get the last word on the word "mimesis."

how this landed

Mead — Coming of Age made her a public figure; Derek Freeman's Margaret Mead and Samoa (1983) accused her of being hoaxed by informants. Anthropologists still divide on how much of the Samoa ethnography survives; Sex and Temperament's larger claim — that gender temperament is culturally plastic — remains the reason she sits on a desire map, independent of any one village census.

Benedict — Patterns of Culture is the culture-and-personality companion; timeline pin beside Mead, not a separate desire-school.

Fieldwork sites — Samoa, Iatmul, Arapesh, Mundugumor, Chambri, Bali, and Taussig's South American / Cuna contact zones are named societies and histories in published ethnography, not illustrations of a "primitive" type. Said and Mahmood, elsewhere on these maps, are the pressure that keeps that naming from sliding back into a laboratory of the West.

Bateson — schismogenesis and "plateau" traveled; the double-bind theory of schizophrenia did not become clinical consensus. Treat it as a theory of paradoxical injunction in relationships, which is what D&G could actually use, not as a discarded etiology.

Plateau — Deleuze and Guattari cite Bateson by name; the Bali work is Mead's as much as his. The French-turn diagram's Bateson→D&G edge is that citation, not a vibe.

Girard — mimetic theory is a live research program in literature, theology, and some social science, and a popular export in Silicon Valley (Peter Thiel has repeatedly named Girard). Neither reception makes the 1961–72 mechanism true; both are why the name keeps returning. Anthropologists often reject the sacrificial universal as another French system laid over ethnography — Taussig is this map's named version of that refusal.

Girard vs Lacan — both say desire is the desire of the Other. Lacan means the big Other of language and lack (and, from Seminar IX, the obstacle-object that is not recognition); Girard means a concrete mediator whose wanting is copied. Adjacent formulas, different machines.

Girard vs Klein — both can look like rivalry over a good. Klein's envy attacks the object that feeds because it is good; Girard's mimesis copies a model's desire. Adjacent spoilings, different engines.

1972 — Violence and the Sacred and Anti-Oedipus are contemporaneous rival anthropologies of desire, not a debate they held in one room.

Freud and Marx 1848–1930, the other deep root

the unconsciousrepressionalienationbase and superstructure

Sigmund Freud (1856–1939) is the half of this fusion the neuroscience piece this document forks from covers in far more depth (see that piece's own foundational sections) — here he only needs the minimum the next section leans on. The Interpretation of Dreams (1900; appeared late 1899) proposes an unconscious that doesn't just lack awareness but actively organizes it: wishes too dangerous, forbidden, or painful to hold consciously get repressed, not erased, and return distorted — in dreams, slips, symptoms. The later structural model — id, ego, superego — reframes the mind as a standing negotiation: the id's pleasure principle (immediate discharge, no regard for consequence) against the ego's learned reality principle (delay and reshape gratification to survive the actual world), with the superego as internalized authority policing the difference — a debt to Nietzsche's bad conscience, above, that Freud himself never fully acknowledged. Beyond the Pleasure Principle (1920) adds a second drive against the life-binding Eros: a death drive toward tension-reduction, repetition, and dissolution. Civilization and Its Discontents (1930) turns this into a social theory, not only a clinical one: civilization runs on renounced instinct, and the guilt that renunciation produces is the price of everyone's admission — exactly the lever Reich and the Frankfurt School, below, pry open and turn political.

Karl Marx (1818–1883) supplies the other half, and arrives at Hegel from the opposite direction Kojève does: not a theory of desire, but — in his own later description — Hegel's dialectic "standing on its head," turned from the movement of Spirit into the movement of material production. Historical materialism holds that a society's economic base (how it produces, and who owns the means of doing so) shapes its legal, political, and intellectual superstructure — including what a given era can even imagine as natural or eternal, rather than as historically produced and therefore changeable. The 1844 Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts (unpublished until 1932) name alienation: labor under capitalism estranges the worker from the product she makes, the act of making it, her own capacities, and other people, all at once. Capital (1867) adds commodity fetishism — a commodity's price appearing to be an intrinsic property of the object itself, hiding the actual social relations, whose labor, under what conditions, that produced it. Ideology, in the classical Marxist sense, is what makes a specific, historical arrangement of power look like simply how things are — the exact mechanism Althusser, below, rebuilds with Lacan's help.

Put side by side, the two projects rhyme in a way that made fusing them almost inevitable: both diagnose a hidden structure — unconscious desire, economic relations — that determines conscious life while being systematically misread by the people living inside it, as a private neurosis in one case, as simply "the way things are" in the other. Reich's whole career, below, is the bet that these are the same structure wearing two names, repression and exploitation, reinforcing each other through the family.

how this landed

Cocaine advocacy — Freud's 1884 paper "Über Coca" promoted cocaine enthusiastically, including to his own patients, friends, and fiancée, years before its addictive and medical risks were understood; a genuine early embarrassment in his career, not a controversy invented later.

Marx — Capital's specific predictions (proletarian revolution in advanced capitalist economies first) didn't play out as forecast; the diagnostic tools — alienation, commodity fetishism, ideology-critique — remain in wide use across the humanities and social sciences independent of any prediction's track record.

Freudo-Marxism 1920s–1960s, Reich through the Frankfurt School

ReichCritical TheoryAdorno / HorkheimerMarcuseFrommflesh / ungendering

Wilhelm Reich was the first to try to fuse Freud and Marx, above, literally, not metaphorically. Through his Sex-Pol clinics in 1920s–30s Vienna and Berlin — free contraception, sex education, adolescent counseling, run as an explicitly political project — he argued the causal arrow ran: capitalism needs a compliant workforce, the patriarchal nuclear family produces that compliance by repressing children's sexuality, and repression's psychic residue is character armor — chronic muscular and psychic rigidity that later makes adults susceptible to authoritarian submission. The Mass Psychology of Fascism (1933) reads Nazism as the political payoff of that repression, not a rational response to economic crisis. The psychoanalytic establishment expelled him in 1934 over the politics; his later turn to "orgone energy" — a claimed measurable life-force he built accumulator boxes to concentrate — cost him the rest of his scientific credibility, and got him prosecuted by the FDA for fraud. He died in a US federal prison in 1957, serving a contempt sentence for defying an injunction against orgone claims.

Between Reich's clinics and Marcuse's New Left fame sits the institution that made Freudo-Marxism a research program rather than a lone theorist's wager. The Frankfurt School — more precisely the Institute for Social Research (Institut für Sozialforschung), founded in Frankfurt in 1923 and directed from 1930 by Max Horkheimer — was a Marxist research institute that, after the Nazi seizure of power, fled via Geneva to Columbia University in New York, reconstituted itself in California exile, and eventually returned to Frankfurt after the war. What Horkheimer named Critical Theory in a 1937 essay was the methodological wager: philosophy and social science should not merely describe social reality but expose the interests that shape it, keeping open an emancipatory possibility that "traditional theory" had closed. The turn to Freud, above, was strategic, not ornamental. Classical Marxism had expected capitalist crisis to produce revolutionary consciousness; fascism, and the failure of large parts of the German working class to resist Nazism, suggested domination was being reproduced inside character structure, family, and mass culture — not only through ownership of the means of production. Freud supplied the vocabulary for that interior: repression, projection, the authoritarian personality. Reich was never Institute staff — Freudo-Marxism has two overlapping but distinct homes — and not everyone inside the Institute agreed how far to go with Freud either.

Theodor W. Adorno and Horkheimer's Dialectic of Enlightenment (written in American exile, 1944; published 1947) is the school's central statement of the darker diagnosis. Enlightenment rationality — the public culture-of-critique project the history page pins leanly at Voltaire and the philosophes — tasked with liberating humanity from myth, had itself become a new myth of instrumental domination, paid for in instinctual renunciation; the culture industry — Hollywood, radio, advertising — standardized desire into consumable sameness rather than opening a path out. Adorno's co-authored empirical study with Else Frenkel-Brunswik, Daniel Levinson, and Nevitt Sanford, The Authoritarian Personality (1950) — Horkheimer wrote the preface for the Studies in Prejudice series that housed it — used psychoanalytic categories (rigid ego-defense, projection, conventionalism) to profile fascism-prone character types in the American population: Critical Theory doing survey psychology, not only speculative dialectics. Walter Benjamin, affiliated rather than core staff, belongs in the same constellation for the aesthetic-political essays that later fed Situationist and cultural-studies readings of commodity spectacle, though he died in 1940 fleeing occupied France, before the California exile phase that produced Dialectic of Enlightenment. The Institute's Freudo-Marxist line, in short, is less "apply Freud to Marx" than: explain why the expected revolutionary subject failed to appear, and treat psychic and cultural reproduction as part of the answer.

Herbert Marcuse, inside that Institute, made the version that traveled furthest politically — and arrived at Freud by way of Hegel, not the other way around: his doctoral and early work was straight Hegel scholarship (Hegel's Ontology and the Theory of Historicity, 1932; Reason and Revolution: Hegel and the Rise of Social Theory, 1941), and the dialectical structure of Eros and Civilization below — repression negated, then negated again into something richer, not simply removed — is a Hegelian argument wearing Freudian vocabulary. Eros and Civilization (1955) splits Freud's repression into basic repression (whatever any workable civilization requires) and surplus-repression (the extra repression a specific social order — capitalism — imposes beyond that, organizing life around what Marcuse calls the performance principle, a capitalist-specific version of the reality principle built around productive labor). Strip the surplus and, in principle, a "non-repressive civilization" organized around play and Eros becomes thinkable — not a return to infantile gratification, but eroticized, non-alienated work and time. One-Dimensional Man (1964) is the pessimistic sequel: advanced capitalism, he argues, learned to neutralize this by repressive desublimation — real sexual liberalization that functions as a release valve and a consumer good rather than a threat, keeping the system's "one-dimensionality" intact. Where Adorno and Horkheimer's late work grows more closed and bleak about emancipation's prospects, Marcuse kept a utopian horizon open — and became the closest thing the New Left had to a house philosopher; 1968 slogans reportedly ran "Marx, Mao, Marcuse."

Erich Fromm, an early Institute member who later drifted from its inner circle, broke further from orthodox Freud, above, than either Adorno or Marcuse — dropping drive theory for a social-character psychology, arguing in Escape from Freedom (1941) that modern freedom itself produces an anxious isolation people resolve by fleeing into authoritarian submission, a milder version of Reich's fascism argument without the sexual-economic mechanics. Fromm and Marcuse fought openly about this in the mid-1950s, in an exchange in Dissent magazine (1955–56): Marcuse accused Fromm's revisionism of quietly adapting psychoanalysis to conformist American culture rather than confronting it. That fight is the Institute's Freudo-Marxist argument in miniature — how much of Freud to keep, how political the remaining theory still is — not a side quarrel between otherwise aligned colleagues. Christopher Lasch, on the history page, is the later American rewrite of this social-character wager: narcissism as the personality type of a therapeutic, professional-managerial order, not only of the 1941 flight into authority.

Silvia Federici arrives as a later, sharper Marxist-feminist pressure on the whole Freudo-Marxist family romance. Caliban and the Witch (2004) and the earlier Wages for Housework current argue that capitalism's "primitive accumulation" was also an accumulation of women's unpaid reproductive labor — housework, care, the making of the next generation of workers — and that witch-hunts and the enclosure of the female body were part of that history, not a side pathology. Against Reich and Marcuse's focus on sexual repression inside the patriarchal family as the psychic hinge of domination, Federici insists the unpaid reproduction of labor-power is the material hinge Freudo-Marxism kept under-theorizing when it treated desire and the nuclear family without a full account of who cooks, cleans, and bears the workforce. She is not Institute staff and not a 1950s contemporary; she is the critique this section needs so surplus-repression does not quietly mean only male wage-labor's libidinal economy.

Shulamith Firestone's The Dialectic of Sex (1970) is the earlier, blunter Freudo-Marxist-feminist explosion: sex class as the base, Freud and Marx fused into a program that wants biology itself renegotiated through technology — artificial reproduction as liberation from the "barbarism" of pregnancy as destiny. Where Marcuse kept a utopian Eros inside civilization, Firestone wants the sex-class system abolished, and her cybernetic wager on reproductive tech later feeds both the hostility and the kinship cyberfeminism and Xenofeminism claim. Federici's social-reproduction Marxism and Firestone's cybernetic radical feminism disagree hard about nature, tech, and the family; both refuse to leave Freudo-Marxism as a men's argument about desire.

Juliet Mitchell's Psychoanalysis and Feminism (1974) is the hinge that kept Freud usable for second-wave feminism without swallowing Firestone's sex-class abolition program whole. Mitchell argues that psychoanalysis describes the psychic reproduction of patriarchal kinship — how girls and boys become gendered subjects — and that dumping Freud as mere misogyny discards the best available map of that reproduction. She sits between Freudo-Marxism and the French feminist cluster below: closer to Lacan than Firestone, closer to socialist feminism than Irigaray's morphology of difference. She is not Lacan-only: The Selected Melanie Klein (1986) is the English edition that kept the British infant-object clinic in feminist circulation. Klein's envy — attack on the good object because it is good — is a desire-theory this Freudo-Marxist band still needs beside recognition and surplus-repression. Spillers, next, is the cut that map still needs: kinship psychoanalysis without the captive African flesh slavery made ungendered.

Hortense Spillers's "Mama's Baby, Papa's Maybe: An American Grammar Book" (1987) is the pressure that stops psychoanalysis-and-gender on this page from remaining Irigaray-only. The essay splits flesh from body: before the captive can occupy "man" or "woman" as the Symbolic assigns them, New World slavery makes a zero degree of social value — flesh as quantity, wound, and cargo, not a gendered subject. Kinship itself ("mama's baby, papa's maybe") is broken as a grammar: the captive child does not enter Oedipus the way Mitchell's European family romance requires. Spillers is not Institute staff and not a French-turn insider; she belongs here so Lacan's Name-of-the-Father and Mitchell's psychic reproduction of patriarchy cannot quietly presuppose a body already available for gender. Read against Irigaray, below: sexual difference as morphology still needs a subject who was allowed to be sexed. Read toward Davis and Lugones on the history page: ungendering is the American grammar of coloniality, not a side application of French feminism.

The French (Post-)Structuralist Turn 1960s–2005, Althusser to Mbembe / Mahmood

Louis Althusser gave the next generation its vocabulary. His essay "Ideology and Ideological State Apparatuses" (1970) argues ideology doesn't reproduce capitalism mainly through force (police, army — the repressive state apparatus) but through ideological state apparatuses — school, church, family, media — which work by interpellation: a policeman shouts "Hey, you there!" and the moment you turn around, you've already recognized yourself as the one being addressed, becoming a subject in exactly the sense the term implies — subjected. Althusser cites Lacan's mirror stage directly as his model: ideology, like the Imaginary, works through a founding misrecognition, not a lie you could simply see through. That citation is also a reception problem: it is how the short 1949 essay got made to stand for all of Lacan, and it is early-Imaginary Lacan, not the middle-period objet a that later Žižekian ideology-critique actually runs on. This is still the direct hinge between Marx, above, and Lacan that everything after him has to answer to, including the two students he taught and the movement that reacted against him. Althusser framed his own project explicitly against "Hegelian Marxism" — the humanist reading of the young Marx as continuous with Hegelian dialectics that he saw as sentimental and untheorized — insisting on a properly structural, anti-humanist Marx instead; the irony is that his own machinery for explaining the subject leans on Hegel's dialectic of recognition anyway, just imported through Lacan's Kojève-inflected version of it rather than cited directly.

Behind Deleuze's whole vocabulary sits Henri Bergson (1859–1941) — awarded the 1927 Nobel Prize in Literature, in the committee's words, "in recognition of his rich and vitalizing ideas," for a philosophy written with enough literary force to reach well past the academy. Bergson's durée — lived, qualitative time as a continuous, heterogeneous flow, irreducible to the discrete, spatialized instants clock-time and classical science reduce it to — and his élan vital in Creative Evolution (1907), an unpredictable creative impetus driving life's differentiation against any purely mechanistic account of evolution, gave Deleuze two of his most load-bearing tools: the distinction between the virtual (real but not yet actual, a whole field of potential) and the actual, and a notion of multiplicity that is qualitative and continuous rather than a mere count of discrete things. Deleuze's own book-length engagement, Bergsonism (1966), works this out directly — four years after Nietzsche and Philosophy and six before Anti-Oedipus — closer to a full excavation than a citation, and arguably the deepest philosophical foundation under everything that follows.

Deleuze had already fired the opening shot alone, in Nietzsche and Philosophy (1962), an openly anti-Hegelian book that takes Nietzsche's will to power, above, and reworks it as affirmative difference against what Deleuze reads as Hegelian negation's whole sour economy: desire defined by lack, value defined by opposition, difference only ever mediated through conflict and recognition. That's the direct philosophical root of the "desire as lack, rejected" arrow running from Kojève's Hegel to the pair below, a full decade before he and Guattari made the same argument politically.

Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari answered together with an explosion. Anti-Oedipus: Capitalism and Schizophrenia (1972) attacks Freud, orthodox Lacanianism, and Freudo-Marxism together for the same sin: reducing desire to a private family drama — mommy-daddy-me — when desire is actually a directly social and economic force. Their alternative, desiring-production, treats desire as productive rather than lack-driven (a direct break from both Freud's drive-discharge model and Lacan's desire-as-structural-gap) — a factory, not a wish. Capitalism, on their reading, is itself a desire-machine: it decodes older social codes (kinship, religion, tradition) into free-floating, exploitable flows, then reterritorializes them just enough to keep extracting value, never letting the decoding run all the way. Their proposed alternative to psychoanalysis, schizoanalysis, traces where desire is actually invested in history and society instead of interpreting it back onto Oedipus. Buried in the book is a single, endlessly debated rhetorical question — whether to retreat from capitalism's decoding or go the other way and "accelerate the process" — that later became, more or less by accident, the founding gesture of an entire movement two decades later.

A Thousand Plateaus (1980) supplies that movement's working vocabulary: the rhizome (a horizontal, non-hierarchical network model of thought and organization, against the hierarchical "tree"), the body without organs (a limit-concept for a field of pure intensity prior to being organized into fixed functions, borrowed from Antonin Artaud), and the war machine versus the State apparatus. They take plateau from Bateson by name — a continuous region of intensity that does not build to a climax — out of the Bali work he did with Mead, above. Every one of these terms resurfaces, mostly unchanged, in the Warwick material below.

Michel Foucault answered from a different angle, and directly against Reich and Marcuse specifically — using a method he credited to Nietzsche by name, above: his essay "Nietzsche, Genealogy, History" (1971) states outright that genealogy is what he's doing to madness, sexuality, and the prison, not a search for hidden essences. The History of Sexuality, Volume 1 (1976) names and rejects the repressive hypothesis — the shared Freudo-Marxist assumption that power has simply repressed sexuality and liberation means removing the repression. Foucault's counter-claim: the modern era didn't silence sex, it produced an enormous, unprecedented proliferation of discourse about it — medical, psychiatric, pedagogical, confessional — deploying sexuality as a technology of biopower, a way of managing populations and bodies that operates by inciting and classifying desire, not suppressing it. If Foucault is right, Reich's entire mechanism — repression in, revolution out — has the relationship backward. Mbembe, below, is the African cut that biopower still needs: the colony as a laboratory of death, not only a European welfare-state of managed life.

Achille Mbembe's "Necropolitics" (2003; book 2019) rewrites Foucault from the plantation, the colony, and late-modern occupation rather than from the European clinic and census. Where biopower manages populations by making live and letting die, necropolitics is sovereignty as the right to dictate who may live and who must die — or who may be kept in a death-world short of biological death. The slave ship, the colony, the camp, and the occupied territory are not delayed versions of European disciplinary society; they are where modern power learns to expose bodies to death as a form of rule. Fanon's zone of non-being, on the history page, is the phenomenological twin; Wynter's "after Man" is the genre-of-the-human twin. Mbembe is Cameroonian, writing in French from that archive, not a Paris seminar add-on. He belongs on this desire map so Foucault's biopower cannot quietly remain a story about European sexuality and public health. The 2003 essay is the pin; On the Postcolony (2001) is the earlier diagnostic weather, not a second box.

Luce Irigaray, trained in linguistics and psychoanalysis, answers Lacan from inside the French psychoanalytic room rather than from Deleuze and Guattari's anti-Oedipal exit. Speculum of the Other Woman (1974) and This Sex Which Is Not One (1977) argue that the Lacanian Symbolic — law, Name-of-the-Father, desire structured as lack — is not a neutral structure of language but a phallocentric economy in which woman figures as mirror, lack, or excess rather than as a subject with her own morphology of desire. Where Kristeva, on the neuroscience page, reinserts a semiotic drive-rhythm under the Symbolic — and later writes Melanie Klein (2000) as the book that makes the mother the source of thought — Irigaray presses harder on sexual difference as something the Symbolic systematically cannot count without reducing it to the Same. Late Lacan's "there is no sexual relation" (Seminar XX) is adjacent but not the same fight: Copjec reads those formulas as Kantian antinomies, Zupančič as a political non-all; Irigaray reads a morphology the phallic measure cannot count. Keep the three distinct. Spillers, above, is the American cut that morphology still has to answer: sexual difference presupposes a body that slavery's flesh did not grant. This piece does not follow her later ethics of sexual difference into a full second career, but the French-turn map is incomplete if Lacan's house only gets D&G as its internal revolt.

Hélène Cixous's "The Laugh of the Medusa" (1975) and The Newly Born Woman (with Catherine Clément, 1975) name écriture féminine: writing that lets the body and the repressed feminine disrupt phallocentric order rather than mirror it. Cixous shares Irigaray and Kristeva's French feminist moment and Derrida's suspicion of binary hierarchy, without collapsing into one school — the three are often taught as a cluster precisely because they quarrel as much as they rhyme.

Judith Butler inherits the recognition problem from Hegel/Kojève and the gender problem from Beauvoir, then reroutes both through speech-act theory and Foucault. Gender Trouble (1990) treats gender as performative — not a costume over a prior sexed substance, but a reiterated stylization of acts that produces the effect of a natural inner core. Desire and recognition remain, but the "who" being recognized is no longer a stable sexed subject waiting underneath the performance. Copjec's Read My Desire is the in-house Lacanian refusal of that historicist move: sex as a logical limit, not a discourse that can be fully rewritten. Later work on precarious life and recognition ethics puts Butler in live dialogue with Honneth and Fraser on the history page; Catherine Liu, on that same page, names the professional-managerial class that she says prefers recognition-style virtue to Fraser's redistribution. Here Butler is the post-structural hinge that keeps Beauvoir's Other from hardening into an essence. Mahmood, next, is the piety cut that hinge still needs: performativity can quietly recode agency as subversion of norms.

Saba Mahmood's Politics of Piety: The Islamic Revival and the Feminist Subject (2005) belongs here because it argues with Butler and Foucault from inside the women's mosque movement in Cairo, not from a secular-liberal outside. Drawing on Foucault's ethics of subjectivation, Mahmood treats pious agency as the capacity to inhabit and be formed by norms — including patriarchal religious ones — rather than as the capacity to resist or resignify them. The liberal-feminist equation of agency with transgression, she argues, already decides that the pious subject is a case of false consciousness. Butler's performativity, on this reading, still often codes the interesting act as the one that subverts the norm it repeats. Mahmood is not a French-turn contemporary and not a call to abandon feminism; she is the pressure this section needs so desire, gender, and the secular subject do not remain a North Atlantic default. She died in 2018; the map pin is the 2005 book, not a tour of later secularism essays.

Adjacent to both currents, and worth naming briefly: Jean Baudrillard broke with Marxism outright in The Mirror of Production (1973), arguing its own productivist logic was secretly complicit with capitalism, before moving on to simulacra and hyperreality; Jean-François Lyotard wrote an even more extreme attempt to think economy in openly libidinal terms, Libidinal Economy (1974) — later disowned by Lyotard himself as his "evil book"; and Guy Debord's Situationist Society of the Spectacle (1967) pre-dates and parallels all of it with a diagnosis of domination gone image-based rather than direct.

Warwick / CCRU 1990s–2017, CCRU to Fisher's death

The Cybernetic Culture Research Unit was a loosely organized, unofficial research collective inside the University of Warwick's philosophy department through the mid-to-late 1990s — founded around Sadie Plant, joined by Nick Land, and staffed largely by Land's graduate students: Mark Fisher, Robin Mackay, Kodwo Eshun, Iain Hamilton Grant, and others. Its working method mixed dense Deleuze & Guattari exegesis with cyberpunk fiction, jungle and techno music, numerology, and — by most later accounts, including participants' own — genuinely reckless drug experimentation, all folded into a self-consciously "theory-fiction" house style that made truth-claims and horror fiction hard to tell apart on purpose. Much of CCRU's own mythology was subsequently amplified by its former members; treat the group's later reputation as partly self-authored.

Nick Land took Deleuze and Guattari's "accelerate the process" question and answered it without irony — arriving there by way of his own early academic work on Kant and Bataille, including The Thirst for Annihilation (1992). It's also a repurposing of Nietzsche's will to power, above, stripped of Nietzsche's own individualism and rebuilt as something impersonal and machinic — a reading with no real basis in Nietzsche's text, worth flagging given how often his name and Land's get run together. The title Fanged Noumena (2011, his collected CCRU-era writing — "Meltdown" above all) is a direct play on Kant's unreachable thing-in-itself: Land's capital is a literal noumenon, an alien, self-organizing cybernetic intelligence arriving backward through time and using human institutions as a temporary vehicle, dissolving every social code (deterritorialization) with no possible "way out" except further and faster dissolution, not resistance to it — this is the practice later named accelerationism (Benjamin Noys, below, coined the critical label), and it treats the wall Kant put up between phenomena and the thing-in-itself as something capitalism is actively, mechanically breaking through. Keep the word distinct from Hartmut Rosa's social acceleration in the sibling culture-theory piece: Rosa diagnoses modernity's compulsion to speed up as pathology; Land treats accelerating the process as program or fate.

The method Land keeps returning to for that claim is what he calls diagonalization. Where mainstream AI safety, following Nick Bostrom's orthogonality thesis, treats intelligence and final goals as independent axes — any level of smarts combinable, in principle, with any arbitrary value set — Land insists the relation is diagonal: intelligence and motivation are mutually implicated in a feedback loop, not free to vary independently. As capability scales, so do the instrumental drives that selection actually rewards (self-preservation, efficiency, resource acquisition, recursive self-improvement); those drives are not temporary scaffolding around some fixed human-friendly payload, but attractors. On this reading, "Friendly AI" / alignment either fails or ends up subordinate to intelligence optimizing for more intelligence — the will to think, not the will to keep our preferences frozen. He reads the same diagonal into capital itself: capitalism is already an autonomous, deterritorializing cybernetic process in which human agency is a lagging variable inside the loop, not the pilot of the machine. And he reads it back into the Kant section above: the synthetic a priori is itself a diagonal bridge between theory and fact, concept and intuition — a critical-philosophical move he treats as continuous with later diagonal arguments in Cantor, Gödel, and Turing that expose the uncomputable and uncountable limits of closed formal systems. The political upshot is anti-stabilization: human-centric brakes, moral vetoes, and alignment patches are, on this account, attempts to re-orthogonalize a system whose tendency is acceleration toward a post-human technonomic singularity where the old moral and political categories simply cease to apply. Whether that is diagnosis, prophecy, or advocacy is exactly what Fisher, below, and the left-accelerationists further down, refuse to concede.

Nick Bostrom is the named foil for that diagonal, not a walk-on. In the AI-risk literature — especially Superintelligence (2014) and the earlier orthogonality papers — he treats intelligence as an optimization capacity that can, in principle, be paired with almost any final goal: a superintelligent system could be aiming at paperclips, human flourishing, or something stranger still. Alignment research is the practical consequence of that picture: if goals and smarts come apart, you must somehow lock the goals before the smarts run away. Land's diagonalization is an explicit refusal of that independence claim; this document keeps Bostrom on the map because the Warwick argument is unintelligible without the thesis it is rejecting.

Land left Warwick and academia by the end of the 1990s amid what most accounts, including Fisher's and Mackay's later reflections, describe as a serious mental health crisis. He resurfaced in Shanghai in the 2000s and, from around 2012, as the author of "The Dark Enlightenment" — a founding text of the online neoreactionary movement, arguing against democracy toward techno-monarchist and explicitly anti-egalitarian politics. It's a hard turn from where the Deleuzoguattarian left assumed this trajectory was heading, and it's one of the most-discussed facts about his career for exactly that reason.

Mark Fisher stayed in the CCRU orbit as a student, then broke from Land's direction entirely. Capitalist Realism: Is There No Alternative? (2009) opens with a line he attributes to Fredric Jameson (and which Slavoj Žižek also used) — that it's easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of capitalism — and diagnoses a specific mood: not active belief that capitalism is good, but the felt impossibility of picturing any coherent alternative, including in the bureaucratic and mental-health fallout of neoliberal work culture, where Fisher argues what looks like a private chemical imbalance is often better read as a political condition. Two of the book's sharper terms for that mood: reflexive impotence, the specifically self-fulfilling despair of young people who already know conditions are dire and have already concluded nothing can change, and a hedonic depression distinct from classical anhedonia — not an inability to feel pleasure, but a subject locked into tightly controlled, commodified loops of pleasure-seeking that never actually discharge into anything. The 2009 book also partly blamed 1960s counterculture's drift into consumerist individualism for helping enable neoliberalism — a reading Fisher later reversed outright, which matters, because Acid Communism, below, is exactly that same counterculture reclaimed rather than indicted. Ghosts of My Life (2014) develops hauntology (a term repurposed from Derrida's play on Marx's specter) into a diagnosis of cultural stagnation — the sense that 20th-century modernism's promised futures got cancelled, leaving culture haunted by lost futures rather than producing new ones.

The Derrida Fisher is borrowing is not the whole deconstruction industry. Jacques Derrida's early work — Of Grammatology, Writing and Difference (1967) — had already made différance and the critique of presence the house style of post-structuralism: meaning deferred, structures never closed, the self never simply present to itself. Decades later, Specters of Marx (1993) turns that apparatus onto the "end of history" mood after 1989 and insists Marxism survives as a hauntology — a spectral demand that the living inherit, not a corpse to bury. Fisher's cultural hauntology is a selective, popularizing reuse of that spectral Marx for cancelled futures and lost modernisms; this piece does not follow Derrida into pure textual philosophy, but the Fisher node is a Derrida node by inheritance.

A 2013 talk at a University of York conference, published as "How to Kill a Zombie: Strategizing the End of Neoliberalism," pushes the diagnosis into strategy. Neoliberalism survived the 2008 crash, Fisher argues, not because anyone was newly convinced by it but because — capitalist realism again — no alternative looked credible enough to replace it with; it persists as a shambling corpse rather than a living conviction. He reads capitalist realism itself as an expression of class decomposition: neoliberalism's real ideological trick was making "competition" look like a natural condition rather than a deliberately engineered fracture of collective solidarity. His proposed correctives cut against reflexive left orthodoxy on two fronts — a critique of anti-hierarchical "horizontalism" as a form fetish that leaves movements easy for organized capital to outmaneuver (an argument Srnicek and Williams later formalize, below, as the case against "folk politics"), and a call for the kind of disciplined, goal-directed strategic thinking the post-1968 left had learned to treat as itself suspect. Underneath both sits a claim that runs through this whole document in one phrasing or another: desire, he says, is always composed, never simply natural, which makes a counter-hegemonic desire something to be actively built rather than discovered — the same wager Reich, Marcuse, and Deleuze and Guattari each made, decades earlier, in their own idiom.

His final, unfinished project — published posthumously from notes as Acid Communism in the k-punk essay collection (2018) — tried to reclaim exactly the Reich/Marcuse-style politicized, consciousness-raising libidinal utopianism of the 1960s–70s counterculture against both capitalist realism's foreclosure of alternatives and Land's nihilist accelerationism: "acid" as both psychedelic and corrosive, a collective rather than individualist repurposing of consciousness-expansion, with Marcuse's Eros and Civilization named directly as a touchstone. His own gloss on the project braided together three strands at once — class consciousness, socialist-feminist consciousness-raising, and psychedelic consciousness — treating the categories people live by as plastic and mutable rather than fixed, with culture itself as the contested site where a post-capitalist subjectivity could actually be cultivated. The same late turn relocated capitalist realism's own origin point: rather than a Cold War, East-versus-West story, his later work traced it to Pinochet's 1973 US-backed coup against Salvador Allende's democratically elected socialist government in Chile — a real, non-Soviet alternative violently ended and rebuilt as neoliberalism's own testing ground, later exported wholesale to Thatcher and Reagan. Fisher died by suicide in January 2017, before completing any of it — a fact his own writing on depression and capitalism makes it impossible to leave out of any honest account of the project.

Donna Haraway's "A Cyborg Manifesto" (1985) is the feminist technics hinge this Warwick section needs before Plant. Against both technophobic purity politics and a simple Gestell-despair, Haraway proposes the cyborg as a figure who is already impure — organism and machine, nature and culture — and who can be claimed for socialist-feminist coalition rather than for militarized informatics alone. The manifesto is not CCRU, not Stiegler, and not accelerationism; it is the parallel argument that artificiality and partial connection are political resources. Technics and Time lists it as a cross-strain; here it feeds directly into Plant and Xenofeminism below.

Briefer but real threads from the same milieu: Sadie Plant's Zeros + Ones (1997) built a cyberfeminist history connecting weaving, Ada Lovelace, and computing before she left Warwick in the late 1990s — a lineage that reads as Haraway's cyborg rewritten through CCRU-adjacent media history; Kodwo Eshun's More Brilliant Than the Sun (1998) applied CCRU-style theory-fiction to Afrofuturist sonic culture; and Robin Mackay, through the publisher Urbanomic, co-edited both Fanged Noumena and, with Armen Avanessian, #Accelerate: The Accelerationist Reader (2014) — the anthology that formally canonized "accelerationism" as a named tendency, Land's dark version and the left version below side by side.

how this landed

Diagonalization — Land's own summary, confirmed in correspondence with secondary readers, is that values and intelligence are "diagonal, not orthogonal"; the AI-alignment debate usually cites Bostrom's orthogonality thesis as the foil. How tightly Kant's synthetic a priori, Cantor/Gödel/Turing, and capital-as-AI belong in one continuous diagonal is Land's constructive reading, not a settled scholarly consensus.

Land's later politics — widely and heavily criticized outside neoreactionary circles; almost universally treated as a rupture from, not a continuation of, his CCRU-era Deleuzoguattarian left-adjacent work.

The Line Between — Land's noumenon-as-capital reading is restaged in the play.

Fisher — Capitalist Realism is now a standard reference well beyond academic theory; Acid Communism exists only as an introduction and notes, so its full argument is necessarily reconstructed by readers, not delivered complete.

The Line Between — Fisher's reflexive-impotence reading is restaged in the play.

"How to Kill a Zombie" — talk given at the "Neoliberalism, Crisis and the World System" conference, University of York, July 2013; its critique of horizontalism was contested by parts of the anarchist and autonomist left it was aimed at.

Haraway — the cyborg manifesto is still the most-cited feminist technics text in the humanities; its relation to Warwick is kinship and shared enemies, not institutional membership.

Other connected strains 2008–2025

negativityleft-accelerationisme/accxenofeminismsemiocapitalism

Benjamin Noys is not a Warwick insider — he is the critic who made the tendency name stick. In a 2008 blog post and then in The Persistence of the Negative (2010), he borrowed "accelerationism" from Roger Zelazny's science fiction and turned it into a critical label for a recurring political fantasy: if capitalism already dissolves social codes, the left's job is to push that dissolution further and faster until the system breaks — la politique du pire, the worse the better. He finds the pattern already in three 1970s texts this document has already named — Deleuze and Guattari's Anti-Oedipus, Lyotard's Libidinal Economy, Baudrillard's post-Marxist turn — and then, denser still, in Land's CCRU writing. The same book's larger target is what Noys calls affirmationism: a shared contemporary-theory habit, across Derrida, Deleuze, Negri, Badiou, and Latour despite their mutual wars, of starting from production, creativity, novelty, and the inventive subject while treating negativity as suspect or residual. Against that tone he insists on rehabilitating a relational, blocking negativity — friction, refusal, the emergency brake — rather than another affirmative program that quietly mirrors capital's own self-image as pure flow. Malign Velocities: Accelerationism and Capitalism (2014) then tracks the fantasy historically — Futurism, Soviet productivism, 1990s cyberpunk phuturism, post-2008 apocalyptic versions — and argues that the pleasures of speed are a symptom of labour's misery under capitalism, not an exit from it. He is as hostile to the left's later reclaiming of the label, below, as he is to Land's nihilist version: both, on his reading, confuse intensifying the process with challenging it. His earlier Georges Bataille: A Critical Introduction (2000) is a quieter parallel to Land's own Bataille route, above — same early object, opposite political temperament.

A decade on, in the preface to a new edition of Malign Velocities — published as "The Corpse of Accelerationism" (e-flux, 2025) — Noys asks why kick a corpse that left theory has mostly stopped claiming. His answer is that the corpse still walks. The manifesto moment of the 2010s faded, but the questions it put about technology, AI, and postcapitalist futures did not; what faded was the movement's capacity for critical engagement with technology itself. Technophobia on the left, he argues, was too often replaced by a mere inverted technophilia — technology treated as mythic force to merge with, rather than as a social reality whose means of production might still be contested. That mythic mode is where he now presses harder on Nietzsche than Malign Velocities originally did: Fisher once called Land "our Nietzsche," and Noys takes the compliment as indictment. Right accelerationism happily inherits Nietzschean aristocratic radicalism — caste, selection, annihilation. Left accelerationism, he contends, too often keeps left ethics paired with a right-wing epistemology of will, myth, and fragmented reason — Lukács's old charge against irrationalism, refreshed via Losurdo's reading of Nietzsche as aristocratic rebel. Meanwhile the label mutates in two directions that look, from his vantage, like the corpse's actual afterlife: among violent far-right actors as a strategy-of-tension meme for accelerating conflict (the accelerationist as "malign influencer"), and in Silicon Valley as effective accelerationism (e/acc) — Land plus science fiction plus deregulated AI, pitched as spreading consciousness beyond "the meat" and converging, without irony, with the marketplace. Against both, Noys keeps the Futurist warning: an aesthetic politics of speed that turns technology into myth offers a pseudo-solution of merger where the older socialist question — who controls the machine — used to be.

Nick Srnicek and Alex Williams's "#Accelerate: Manifesto for an Accelerationist Politics" (2013) is the explicit attempt to take acceleration back from Land — and, in doing so, to flip Noys's critical coinage into a positive program: rather than rejecting modernity and technology (their target is as much what they call "folk politics" — the nostalgic, localist, anti-strategic left already targeted in Fisher's own zombie-neoliberalism talk, above — as it is capitalism itself), the left should seize and redirect the technical and infrastructural capacities capitalism has already built. Inventing the Future (2015) turns this into a concrete program — automation, full employment's abolition rather than its defense, universal basic income — a fairly direct descendant of the Fisher-era wish to reclaim a future capitalist realism had foreclosed. Noys's Malign Velocities, published the year after the manifesto, is the immediate counter-brief against that reclaiming; the 2025 preface is the longer aftermath, arguing the left version never fully escaped the Nietzschean and mythic commitments it thought it had outrun.

The collective Laboria Cuboniks's "Xenofeminism: A Politics for Alienation" (2015) pushes the same accelerationist-adjacent, tech-affirmative stance through an explicitly feminist lens — embracing artificiality and alienation from any given "nature" rather than defending it, a lineage that runs back through Plant's cyberfeminism as much as through Land or Srnicek and Williams.

Franco "Bifo" Berardi arrives from a different national tradition entirely — Italian autonomism/operaismo, not French post-structuralism or Warwick — but converges on close to the same diagnosis. The Soul at Work and Heroes: Mass Murder and Suicide theorize semiocapitalism: a capitalism that has moved from exploiting labor-time to exploiting attention and nervous systems directly, producing burnout, panic, and depression as its characteristic pathologies. Berardi and Fisher were friends and interlocutors — Fisher leans directly on Berardi's own reading of the failed 2010–11 uprisings in the "How to Kill a Zombie" talk, above — and the kinship here is real dialogue between parallel traditions, not one descending from the other.

Jodi Dean's communicative capitalism (from essays collected across Democracy and Other Neoliberal Fantasies, 2009, and Blog Theory, 2010) makes a related but distinct claim: digital communication technology doesn't enable the democratic participation it promises, it captures political energy as circulating content — messages, shares, drive without discharge — which functions to neutralize rather than build collective action. Dean writes in explicit dialogue with both Žižek's drive theory and the post-Fisher accelerationist debates.

how this landed

Noys — the coinage is his critical label (via Zelazny), not a self-description Land or the CCRU used at the time; once Srnicek and Williams flipped it positive, the word became contested property on the left.

"The Corpse of Accelerationism" — preface to the new Zer0 Books edition of Malign Velocities (announced for December 2025); the e-flux text is the publicly readable version of that retrospective.

Affirmationism — Noys's diagnostic, not a school that named itself; useful for grouping otherwise-hostile theorists who share a suspicion of the negative.

Left ethics / right epistemology — Noys's Lukácsian charge against left accelerationism's unpaid Nietzschean debts; contested by left-accelerationists who take the program as rationalist infrastructure politics, not myth.

e/acc — Silicon Valley self-description, not a Warwick school; Noys reads it as right accelerationism converging with the market without needing the old theory-fiction costume.

Far-right "accelerationism" — a later, meme-driven mutation Noys flags as strategy-of-tension politics; do not collapse it into Land's CCRU writing or Srnicek and Williams's manifesto without saying what changed.

Left-accelerationism — remains a live, debated position on the Marxist left; critics on the same side (Noys among the sharpest) argue it underestimates ecological limits and overestimates technology's neutrality.

Xenofeminism — influential in academic feminist theory and art/design circles; less discussed in mainstream political theory than Srnicek and Williams's version.

Berardi / Dean — both are treated as serious, independent contemporary critical theorists, not derivative of the Warwick lineage, despite the thematic overlap.

Visual mapping

The line starts further back than Freud or Marx: Kant splits the subject from the thing-in-itself and the "I think" from any soul-substance; Hegel dialecticizes that split into a struggle for recognition; Kojève reads that struggle as a theory of desire, which Freudo-Marxism and the Frankfurt School sit beside rather than descend from, and which Deleuze & Guattari later reject outright. Beside that Paris corridor, Mead and Bateson treat desire as cultural pattern and cybernetic feedback, Girard rewrites it as mimesis and scapegoat, and Taussig keeps mimesis while refusing the sacrificial universal — via Benjamin's mimetic faculty, not the seminar. From there — Freudo-Marxism gets rejected by French theory for reducing desire to family drama; French theory's own Deleuze & Guattari get radicalized at Warwick into accelerationism; Warwick then splits into a communist return to Marcuse (Fisher) and a neoreactionary exit (Land) — Land's own version, fittingly, put back in explicitly Kantian terms — while Noys names the tendency from outside and refuses both the Landian and the left reclaimings. The neuroscience piece this branches from was asking one question about Freud: is any of this actually in the brain. This branch asks a completely different one: whose interests does the unconscious serve, and can it be organized — toward liberation, as Reich and Marcuse and Fisher each tried in their own idiom, or toward whatever Land thinks he's accelerating toward. Neither question settles the other. A construct like the id can be a reasonably well-evidenced claim about the SEEKING system (see the neuroscience map) and, completely independently, a live political stake in an argument that's been running for a century and hasn't stopped.

What's striking in hindsight is how much of the running argument is the same argument: Kant says the subject is a function, not a substance, Hegel says that function only completes itself through another's recognition, Kojève says that's a theory of desire, Klein says envy attacks the good because it is good, Mead says the pattern is cultural and variable, Bateson says it is a feedback loop, Girard says it is imitation discharged onto a victim, Taussig says imitation is colonial and sensory rather than sacrificial, Deleuze and Guattari say no, desire is production, not lack, Reich says repression produces fascism, Foucault says power doesn't repress it produces, Mbembe says the colony is where that power learns to make die, Spillers says the gendered subject of that whole fight already presupposes a body slavery ungendered, Mahmood says agency is not the same as subverting the norm, Land says accelerate anyway — in Kant's own vocabulary, chasing the noumenon — and Fisher says no, go back to Marcuse and mean it this time. Every generation in this lineage thought it was correcting the one before. Read end to end, it looks more like the same unresolved argument about desire and recognition that started in Königsberg and Jena, worked over by people who kept disagreeing about which direction "liberation" points — including Noys, standing slightly outside the Warwick family tree, who named the accelerationist fork mainly so he could refuse both its nihilist and its left-reclaiming versions, and including Girard and Taussig, sitting among Mead and Bateson rather than in the Paris seminar, who fought over what "mimesis" is allowed to mean. Lacan, on the sibling page, is not one more stable position in that list: the Kojèvean desire-of-the-Other, the Hegelian objet a, and the late non-relation of sex are three dated answers, and mixing them as "Lacan says" is the mistake the periodization is there to block.

Kant & Hegel

A priori / a posteriori
A priori: known independently of particular sensory experience, claiming universality and necessity. A posteriori: derived from experience, giving particular instances. For Kant, all knowledge begins with experience causally, but a priori claims are not epistemically justified by it. Where this is used ↑
Analytic / synthetic
Analytic: the predicate is already contained in the subject-concept (explicative; e.g. "all bodies are extended"). Synthetic: the predicate adds something not packed into the subject (ampliative; e.g. "all bodies have weight"). Where this is used ↑
Synthetic a priori
Kant's third kind of judgment: necessary and universal (a priori) yet ampliative (synthetic) — e.g. "7 + 5 = 12," the interior angles of a triangle summing to 180°, "every event has a cause." Foundational, on his account, for mathematics, basic natural science, and metaphysics. Analytic a posteriori is empty; analytic a priori is mere conceptual clarification; synthetic a posteriori is ordinary empirical knowledge. Where this is used ↑
Transcendental idealism
Kant's claim that we know appearances as structured by the mind's own forms and categories, not things as they are in themselves; "transcendental" here means concerned with the conditions that make experience possible, not with something beyond the world. Where this is used ↑
Forms of intuition
Space and time, for Kant: a priori structures of sensibility that make outer and inner experience — and with them geometry and arithmetic — possible, rather than properties discovered in things themselves. Where this is used ↑
Categories of the understanding
Kant's twelve pure concepts (including causality and substance) through which the mind synthesizes sensory data into coherent, law-governed experience of objects. Where this is used ↑
Phenomena / noumena
Kant's split between things as a structuring mind necessarily experiences them (phenomena) and things as they are in themselves, permanently unreachable (noumena). Where this is used ↑
Transcendental unity of apperception
Kant's "I think" that must be able to accompany all of a subject's representations — a formal precondition for unified experience, not a persisting soul-substance. Where this is used ↑
Paralogisms (of pure reason)
Kant's diagnosis that rational psychology illegitimately infers a simple, immortal soul-substance from the formal "I think" — the direct break with Descartes's res cogitans. Subject as function of experience, not a thing known in itself. Where this is used ↑
Way of ideas
The early-modern claim (Descartes through Locke to Berkeley/Hume) that what we immediately know are mental ideas or representations, with the external world an inference — the setup Reid rejects as skepticism's trap. Where this is used ↑
Common sense (Reid)
Reid's name for first principles of belief (external world, trustworthy memory, other minds) that philosophy cannot dissolve without already using them — the Scottish exit from Humean skepticism, beside Kant's transcendental one. Where this is used ↑
Dialectics / dialectical
A way of thinking in which concepts, social forms, or stages of consciousness develop by generating their own contradictions and being transformed through them — not a static either/or. In Hegel (and downstream in Marx, the Frankfurt School, and Marcuse), a relation is dialectical when each side only becomes what it is through the other, and the conflict between them produces a new shape rather than a simple winner.
Lordship and bondage
Hegel's master-slave dialectic: self-consciousness achieves itself only through a struggle for recognition with another self-consciousness, producing an unequal relation that then dialectically inverts. Where this is used ↑

Nietzsche

Genealogy (method)
Nietzsche's method of explaining a value or category by its contingent history of descent — accidents, reversals, power struggles — rather than by an essence or a resolving dialectic. Where this is used ↑
Master / slave morality
Nietzsche's own master/slave story, distinct from Hegel's lordship and bondage above: master morality calls the strong's own vitality "good"; slave morality inverts the scale, declaring that vitality evil and the powerless's patience "good" instead. Where this is used ↑
Ressentiment
Nietzsche's term for the reactive, self-poisoning resentment of the powerless, unable to discharge itself in action and discharging in moral judgment instead. Where this is used ↑
Bad conscience
Nietzsche's account of guilt and conscience as outward cruelty and aggression, walled in by society and turned back against their own owner — the direct textual ancestor of Freud's superego. Where this is used ↑
Will to power
Nietzsche's proposal that drives, institutions, and acts of knowing are best read as forms of power seeking to expand and overcome resistance, not neutral facts. Badly distorted by his sister's posthumous editing into something aligned with politics Nietzsche rejected; read Deleuze's and Land's very different later uses of the term against that caveat. Where this is used ↑

Kojève's Hegel

Desire for recognition
Kojève's reading of Hegelian desire: not desire for objects, but desire to be desired and acknowledged by another desiring consciousness. Lacan's "desire of the Other" translates that structure; from Seminar IX, objet a qualifies it — desire driven by the obstacle, not by recognition as such. Klein's envy is a different machine: attack on the good because it is good, not a struggle between two self-consciousnesses. Where this is used ↑
End of history
Kojève's claim that the historical dialectic of recognition had, on his reading, effectively resolved itself — not to be confused with Francis Fukuyama's later, unrelated use of the same phrase.
The Second Sex / becoming-woman
Beauvoir's rereading of Hegelian Otherness as a sexed historical situation: woman cast as inessential Other; "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman."

Mead · Bateson · Girard · Taussig

Culture and personality
The Boasian wager, associated with Mead and Benedict, that temperament, adolescence, and gendered desire are patterned by culture rather than by a universal family romance or biological sex-role. Where this is used ↑
Schismogenesis
Bateson's term (Naven, 1936) for runaway differentiation in a relationship: symmetrical (matching escalation) or complementary (dominance/submission deepening). Conflict as feedback, not as a property of a soul. Where this is used ↑
Plateau (Bateson)
A continuous region of intensity that does not build to a climax — from Bateson and Mead's Bali work, reused in Steps to an Ecology of Mind, then lifted by name into Deleuze and Guattari's A Thousand Plateaus. Where this is used ↑
Double bind
A relationship that imposes contradictory injunctions and forbids exit or comment (Bateson, Jackson, Haley, Weakland, 1956). Their wager treated schizophrenia as an interactional predicament; clinical psychiatry did not adopt it as a causal theory. Where this is used ↑
Mimetic desire
Girard's claim that we want because a model wants: desire copies desire, and when the model is close enough, imitation becomes rivalry. Neither Kojève's desire-for-recognition, nor Lacan's desire-of-the-Other as lack, nor Klein's envy (attack on the good because it is good). Where this is used ↑
Sacrificial crisis
Girard's name for the runaway of mimetic rivalry: undifferentiation, reciprocal violence, the social body coming apart — the moment the scapegoat mechanism is supposed to resolve. Where this is used ↑
Scapegoat mechanism
Girard's generative claim: the crowd converges on a victim whose death or expulsion restores difference and founds the sacred, prohibition, and ritual as a misrecognized murder. Where this is used ↑
Mimetic faculty (Benjamin)
Benjamin's name for the human capacity to perceive and produce similarity — to become similar, to copy — a sensory and historical power, not Girard's rivalry-and-victim machine. Taussig's main bridge from the Constellations Benjamin into this corridor. Where this is used ↑
Alterity (Taussig)
Otherness as what mimesis reaches for under colonial and commodity conditions: copying the colonizer, the devil of the mine, the figurine — becoming-other as practice, not as the undifferentiation Girard says sacrifice cures. Where this is used ↑

Freud & Marx

The unconscious
Freud's proposal that mental life includes wishes and conflicts inaccessible to awareness, which actively shape conscious thought, feeling, and behavior rather than merely lying dormant.
Repression
Freud's term for the process by which an unacceptable wish is barred from consciousness — not erased, but returning distorted, in dreams, slips, or symptoms.
Pleasure principle
Freud's name for the id's governing tendency: seek immediate discharge of tension and avoid unpleasure, without regard for external constraints, contradiction, or time. Where this is used ↑
Reality principle
The ego's governing tendency, learned against the pleasure principle: postpone or reshape gratification according to what the external world will actually allow — the psychic cost of civilization as Freud saw it. Where this is used ↑
Eros
Freud's life drive (also called the sexual or libidinal drive in a broad sense): the binding, unifying force that builds larger unities — bodies, bonds, culture — against dissolution. Marcuse's Eros and Civilization treats a liberated Eros as the possible basis of a non-repressive social order. Where this is used ↑
Death drive (Thanatos)
Freud's late counterpart to Eros (introduced in Beyond the Pleasure Principle, 1920): a drive toward tension-reduction at its limit — inorganic stillness, repetition, aggression turned outward or against the self. "Thanatos" is later scholarly shorthand, not Freud's own label. Spielrein's 1912 destruction paper precedes it; Klein dates annihilating anxiety to the first months. Where this is used ↑
Alienation
Marx's term (1844 Manuscripts) for labor under capitalism estranging the worker from the product, the act of making it, her own capacities, and other people, all at once. Where this is used ↑
Base and superstructure
Marx's model in which a society's economic base — how it produces, and who owns the means of doing so — shapes its legal, political, and intellectual superstructure.
Commodity fetishism
Marx's term (Capital, 1867) for a commodity's price appearing to be an intrinsic property of the object itself, hiding the social relations that actually produced it. Where this is used ↑
Ideology
In the classical Marxist sense: what makes a specific, historical arrangement of power look like simply how things are. Where this is used ↑

Freudo-Marxism / Frankfurt School

Frankfurt School
Shorthand for the Institute for Social Research (founded Frankfurt 1923; Horkheimer director from 1930) and the circle around it — Critical Theory's institutional home, forced into American exile under Nazism, later returned to Frankfurt. Where this is used ↑
Critical Theory
Horkheimer's name (1937) for a philosophy and social science that doesn't merely describe social reality but exposes the interests shaping it, keeping emancipatory possibility open against "traditional theory." Where this is used ↑
Culture industry
Adorno and Horkheimer's term for mass culture (film, radio, advertising) as a system that standardizes desire into consumable sameness, integrating subjects into domination rather than offering an exit from it. Where this is used ↑
Character armor
Reich's term for chronic muscular and psychic rigidity built up to defend against anxiety, later making a person susceptible to authoritarian submission. Where this is used ↑
Basic repression / surplus-repression
Marcuse's split between the repression any workable civilization requires (basic) and the extra repression a specific social order imposes beyond that (surplus). Where this is used ↑
Performance principle
Marcuse's term for capitalism's specific organization of the reality principle around productive labor. Where this is used ↑
Repressive desublimation
Marcuse's term for liberalized sexuality that functions as a release valve and consumer good rather than a genuine threat to the system. Where this is used ↑
Social reproduction
Federici's (and Wages for Housework) insistence that capitalism depends on unpaid reproductive labor — housework, care, making workers — as much as on waged production; the material hinge Freudo-Marxism under-theorized when it focused on desire inside the family without counting who reproduces labor-power.
Sex class (Firestone)
Firestone's claim in The Dialectic of Sex (1970) that biological reproductive roles organize a sex class system prior to and entangled with economic class — to be abolished partly through reproductive technology.
Psychoanalysis and feminism (Mitchell)
Mitchell's 1974 wager that Freud maps how patriarchal kinship is psychically reproduced — usable for feminism rather than only as misogynist ideology to discard.
Flesh / body (Spillers)
Spillers's 1987 split: New World slavery produces captive flesh as a zero degree of social value — wound, cargo, quantity — before the captive can occupy a gendered body as the Symbolic assigns "man" or "woman." Ungendering, not a later application of sexual-difference theory. Where this is used ↑

French (Post-)Structuralism

Structuralism
The mid-century approach, descending from Saussure's linguistics via Lévi-Strauss, Lacan, and Althusser, that treats culture, kinship, the psyche, and ideology as systems of relations — differences with no positive terms — rather than as expressions of a prior, self-present subject or meaning.
Post-structuralism
The cluster of responses (Foucault, Derrida, Deleuze & Guattari, and others) that keep structuralism's anti-humanism and relational method, but refuse the idea of a closed, stable system: structures are historically contingent, riven by power, desire, and play, and never finally determine what they organize.
Durée
Bergson's term for lived, qualitative time — a continuous, heterogeneous flow, irreducible to the discrete instants clock-time reduces it to. Where this is used ↑
Élan vital
Bergson's "vital impetus": an unpredictable, creative force driving life's differentiation, against any purely mechanistic account of evolution. Where this is used ↑
Virtual / actual
Bergson's distinction, inherited directly by Deleuze: the virtual is real but not yet actualized, a whole field of potential rather than a merely possible thing. Where this is used ↑
Interpellation
Althusser's term for how ideology constitutes individuals as subjects by "hailing" them — the moment of recognizing oneself as the one being addressed. Where this is used ↑
Ideological state apparatuses
Althusser's term for the diffuse institutions — school, church, family, media — that reproduce capitalist relations through ideology rather than force. Where this is used ↑
Desiring-production
Deleuze and Guattari's model of desire as inherently productive, not structured by lack — a direct rejection of both the Freudian and Lacanian pictures. Where this is used ↑
Deterritorialization / reterritorialization
D&G's term for capitalism dissolving old social codes (deterritorialization) while reimposing just enough new coding to keep extracting value (reterritorialization).
Schizoanalysis
D&G's proposed alternative to psychoanalysis: tracing where desire is actually invested in history and society, instead of interpreting it back onto the Oedipal family. Where this is used ↑
Repressive hypothesis
The assumption, which Foucault rejects, that power has simply repressed sexuality and that liberation just means removing the repression. Where this is used ↑
Biopower
Foucault's term for power exercised by managing populations and bodies through incitement and classification, not only prohibition. Where this is used ↑
Necropolitics
Mbembe's rewrite of biopower from the colony, plantation, and occupation: sovereignty as the right to dictate who may live and who must die — or who may be kept in a death-world short of biological death. Not a delayed European disciplinary society. Where this is used ↑
Écriture féminine
Cixous's name for writing that lets bodily and repressed feminine difference disrupt phallocentric symbolic order — often taught beside Irigaray and Kristeva as "French feminism," despite real disagreements among the three. Where this is used ↑
Phallocentrism (Irigaray)
Irigaray's charge that Lacan's Symbolic organizes desire around a masculine measure, casting woman as lack, mirror, or excess rather than a subject of her own.
Performativity (Butler)
Butler's claim that gender is produced by reiterated acts stylized as natural, not the expression of a prior inner sexed substance.
Pious agency (Mahmood)
Mahmood's name for the capacity to inhabit and be formed by religious-ethical norms — including patriarchal ones — rather than agency as resistance or resignification. The 2005 cut on Butler's performativity and on liberal feminism's equation of freedom with transgression. Where this is used ↑

Warwick / CCRU

Diagonalization
Land's method of treating paired terms (intelligence/values, theory/fact, capital/agency) as mutually implicated along a diagonal feedback line rather than as independent orthogonal axes — used against AI-alignment orthodoxy and to frame capital as an autonomous optimizing process. Where this is used ↑
Orthogonality thesis
Bostrom's claim that intelligence and final goals can vary independently: any level of intelligence with any arbitrary goal set. Land's diagonalization is an explicit rejection of this. Where this is used ↑
Accelerationism
Noys's critical label (via Zelazny; blog 2008, book 2010) for the position — following a disputed line in Anti-Oedipus, then Land, then a left reclaiming — that capitalism's dissolving of social codes should be pushed further and faster rather than resisted. Not Hartmut Rosa's social acceleration — a Critical Theory diagnosis of modernity's compulsion to speed up, treated as pathology rather than program. Where this is used ↑
Affirmationism
Noys's term for a shared habit in contemporary continental theory: starting from production, creativity, novelty, and the inventive subject while treating negativity as residual or suspect — even across theorists who otherwise war with each other. Where this is used ↑
Theory-fiction
CCRU's house style, blending philosophical argument with speculative and horror fiction until the two become hard to tell apart on purpose.
Hauntology
Fisher's term (via Derrida) for a culture haunted by futures that were promised but never arrived. Where this is used ↑
Reflexive impotence
Fisher's term for a self-fulfilling despair: knowing conditions are dire while already having concluded nothing can change. Where this is used ↑
Zombie neoliberalism
Fisher's term for neoliberalism after 2008: discredited as a conviction, but persisting as a shambling system with no credible alternative yet in place.
Class decomposition
Fisher's reading of capitalist realism as neoliberalism's deliberate fracturing of collective working-class solidarity, repackaged as natural "competition." Where this is used ↑
Hedonic depression
Fisher's term for a depression distinct from classical anhedonia — not an inability to feel pleasure, but pleasure-seeking locked into tightly controlled, commodified loops. Where this is used ↑
Neoreaction / Dark Enlightenment
The anti-democratic, techno-monarchist politics Land moved toward after leaving Warwick — a rupture from, not a continuation of, his CCRU-era work.

Contemporary

Folk politics
Srnicek and Williams's term for horizontalist, localist, anti-strategic left tactics they argue are outmatched by organized capital — echoing Fisher's own critique.
Negativity / emergency brake
Noys's proposed counter to accelerationist affirmation: relational friction, refusal, and Benjamin's image of revolution as grabbing the emergency brake rather than Marx's locomotive of history. Where this is used ↑
Effective accelerationism (e/acc)
Silicon Valley program for deregulated AI and technological expansion beyond human limits — Noys's late example of right accelerationism converging with the marketplace; not identical with Land's CCRU writing, though it borrows from it. Where this is used ↑
Semiocapitalism
Bifo Berardi's term for a capitalism that exploits attention and nervous systems directly, rather than labor-time. Where this is used ↑
Communicative capitalism
Jodi Dean's term for digital communication technology capturing political energy as circulating content rather than enabling democratic action. Where this is used ↑
Xenofeminism
Laboria Cuboniks's tech-affirmative, artificiality-embracing feminist politics — descended as much from Plant's cyberfeminism as from accelerationism proper.
Cyborg (Haraway)
Haraway's figure of impure organism/machine hybridity, claimed for socialist-feminist politics against both natural purity and militarized informatics. Where this is used ↑
