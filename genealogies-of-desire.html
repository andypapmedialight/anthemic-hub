Genealogies of Desire

Freud and Marx were fused in the 1920s, blown apart by French theory in the 1970s, remixed into cyberpunk philosophy at a UK university philosophy department in the 1990s, and split again — one branch communist, one branch neoreactionary. The other lineage this project's Freud could have gone down.

NOTE — this branch is intellectual history and political theory, not an empirical grounding project like the neuroscience piece it forks from. "How well-evidenced" isn't really the right axis here; "how influential" and "what it was reacting against" are. Read the margin notes accordingly — they track reception and controversy, not experimental support.

Kant and Hegel 1780s–1830s, the deep root

transcendental unity of apperceptionlordship and bondageGerman Idealism

Everything in this document is downstream of a fight that starts here, well before the twentieth-century rooms that later redistributed it. Kant's Critique of Pure Reason (1781) splits reality into phenomena (things as the mind's own categories — space, time, causality — necessarily structure them) and noumena (things as they are in themselves, permanently unreachable by that structuring). Buried in the same book is a quieter, equally consequential move: the "I think" that must be able to accompany all of a person's representations — the transcendental unity of apperception — is a formal, functional precondition for having unified experience at all, not a persisting soul-substance Descartes' rational psychology assumed it must be. Kant spends the Paralogisms chapter explicitly arguing that inference is illegitimate. That's the same move this whole document keeps running into under different names: a subject as something a system does, not something a system has.

Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit (1807) treats Kant's phenomena/noumena wall as an artifact of not yet being dialectical enough, and replaces the static split with a developmental one: consciousness passes through and overcomes its own successive, self-undermining shapes — sense-certainty, perception, understanding, self-consciousness, reason — on the way to a fuller self-knowledge. Its most consequential single passage for everything that follows is lordship and bondage (the master-slave dialectic): self-consciousness only becomes genuinely self-conscious through a life-and-death struggle for recognition with another self-consciousness, producing an unequal master/slave relation that then dialectically inverts, since the slave's formative labor on the object gives the slave a more genuine self-relation than the master ever gets from simply being obeyed. This is the actual text Kojève will read, a century later, four chapters into a much longer book, as fundamentally a theory of desire.

A separate, stranger Hegel passage — the 1805–06 Jena manuscripts' "night of the world," consciousness as an abyss where disconnected images surface and vanish — becomes, over a century later, Žižek's source for the self-relating negativity covered in the neuroscience piece's Žižek section, and Adrian Johnston's "transcendental materialism" there borrows its name directly from the Kantian sense above: conditions of possibility, not something beyond nature.

how this landed

Kant's Paralogisms — still the standard reference point for "the subject as function, not substance" arguments; both Lacan's and Hofstadter's versions of that claim, in the sibling piece, sit in this lineage whether or not either cites Kant directly.

Lordship and bondage — a small fraction of a very long book that became, largely through Kojève, wildly disproportionate to its own space in the Phenomenology; most Hegel scholars read it as one moment in a longer argument, not the book's real point.

"Night of the world" — a pre-Phenomenology text Hegel never published this way; Žižek's heavy reliance on it is itself contested by some Hegel scholars as reading a mature system back into discarded early drafts.

Nietzsche 1872–1888, genealogy as a method

genealogy as methodmaster / slave moralityressentimentwill to power

This whole document's method has a name, and Nietzsche invented it. On the Genealogy of Morals (1887) doesn't ask what "good" and "evil" really are, in some timeless sense; it asks how those words came to mean what they mean — out of which historical relations of force, serving which interests, at whose expense. That's genealogy in Nietzsche's specific sense: not an origin story that explains a thing by its essence, but a history of descent that explains a thing by the accidents, reversals, and power struggles that produced it, with no guarantee the story resolves anywhere in particular. It's the deliberate opposite of Hegel's dialectic, above: no necessary movement toward a fuller self-knowledge, no reconciliation waiting at the end — just one contingent reversal after another.

The book's central case study is a genealogy of morality itself. Master morality praises what the strong naturally are — vital, assertive, unapologetic — and calls it "good," with "bad" a mere afterthought (whatever isn't that). Slave morality inverts the whole scale: unable to act on their own strength, the powerless perform a revaluation instead, declaring the master's vitality itself evil and their own patience and self-denial "good." Nietzsche's name for the psychological fuel behind that inversion is ressentiment — a reactive, self-poisoning resentment that can't discharge itself in action, so it discharges in judgment instead. It's a strikingly different master/slave story than Hegel's: Hegel's slave gains a more genuine self-relation through labor; Nietzsche's slave wins by a hostile trick of valuation, and Nietzsche thinks the trick, not the labor, is what actually built modern morality.

The Genealogy's second essay gets closer to psychoanalysis than anything else in nineteenth-century philosophy: bad conscience, Nietzsche argues, is what happens when cruelty and aggression that used to discharge outward — in raids, punishment, open violence — get walled in by society and turned back against their own owner. That self-directed cruelty is what conscience, guilt, and the sense of having an "inner depth" actually are: not achievements of civilization, but civilization's violence redirected inward. It's the direct textual ancestor of Freud's superego a few decades later, covered in the neuroscience piece's prehistory section — including Freud's own documented, anxious avoidance of reading Nietzsche closely.

Underneath both moral genealogies sits Nietzsche's will to power: the proposal that every drive, institution, and even every act of knowing is best read as a form of power seeking to expand and overcome resistance, not a neutral fact waiting to be discovered. Two very different later readers pick this up and run in opposite directions. Deleuze's Nietzsche and Philosophy, below, turns it into affirmative difference — force and intensity as positive and productive, irreducible to negation, the direct philosophical weapon against Hegel that Deleuze and Guattari later fire politically. Land's Warwick-era writing, much further below, turns the same vocabulary into something closer to its opposite of Nietzsche's own individualist ideal: an impersonal, machinic force — capital itself — that uses human institutions as a vehicle and answers to no one's self-overcoming but its own.

One more debt is close to a direct citation. Foucault's own essay "Nietzsche, Genealogy, History" (1971) states outright that the method running through his own later work, below, is Nietzschean genealogy applied to modern institutions: no hidden essence of madness, sexuality, or the prison waiting to be uncovered, only a history of the accidents and power struggles that produced the categories in the first place. Foucault's repressive hypothesis, below, is Nietzschean genealogy pointed directly at Reich and Marcuse's account of sexual repression, above.

how this landed

Genealogy as method — now a standard historical and critical technique far beyond Nietzsche's own moral psychology; Foucault's version is the most influential contemporary descendant.

Ressentiment — widely used across critical theory and political commentary, sometimes loosely; Nietzsche's own use is more psychologically specific than its popularized descendants.

Will to power — badly distorted by his sister Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche's posthumous editing (The Will to Power, 1901/1906) into something aligned with nationalist and antisemitic politics Nietzsche explicitly rejected in his own writing; most serious scholarship treats that volume as a compromised, editorially constructed text, not his book.

Land's repurposing — a modern, deliberately impersonal reading of will-to-power vocabulary, not evidence of Nietzsche's own politics; worth keeping those two things separate given how often his name gets attached to whatever borrows his terms.

Kojève's Hegel 1933–1939, the seminar that transmitted him

master-slave dialecticdesire for recognitionthe seminar everyone attended

A century after Hegel, the text above reaches twentieth-century French thought largely through one room in Paris. Alexandre Kojève — Russian-born, eventually a French civil servant, an idiosyncratic reader of Hegel who folded in Marx and Heidegger along the way — ran a seminar on Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit at the École Pratique des Hautes Études from 1933 to 1939 that turned out to be one of the highest-leverage rooms in twentieth-century French thought. The attendance list alone explains a lot of what follows: Jacques Lacan, Georges Bataille, Maurice Merleau-Ponty, Raymond Aron, and Raymond Queneau, who later edited the seminar transcripts into the book most people actually read. Kojève's central move was reading Hegel's master-slave dialectic as a theory of desire itself: desire isn't for objects, it's for recognition by another desiring consciousness, which makes human desire structurally social and reciprocal from the outset, driving a historical dialectic Kojève argued had, in his own reading, effectively reached its end.

Lacan is the direct line into the psychoanalytic material covered in the neuroscience piece this one branches from: his claim that desire is always the desire of the Other is Kojève's recognition-structure translated into psychoanalytic terms. Deleuze and Guattari went the opposite direction — Anti-Oedipus's whole desiring-production apparatus, below, is built explicitly against this Hegelian picture of desire as lack chasing recognition, insisting desire is positive and productive from the start, not a structural absence waiting to be filled. Bataille took the seminar somewhere stranger still: his economy of transgression, expenditure, and sovereignty was in large part a refusal of Kojève's own "end of history" — a search for whatever the closed, fully satisfied post-historical subject couldn't contain — and that refusal runs downstream into Baudrillard's symbolic exchange and the Situationist critique of spectacle, below. Merleau-Ponty took a third route entirely, into phenomenology proper, a tributary this piece doesn't follow further.

how this landed

Kojève — historians of philosophy generally treat this reading as more Kojève than Hegel: brilliant and hugely generative, but a real departure from the text, not a neutral commentary.

Attendance list — well documented, since the seminar was recorded and later edited by Queneau; how directly each attendee was actually shaped by it, versus absorbing it secondhand, is debated case by case.

"End of history" — Kojève's own version predates, and differs substantially from, Francis Fukuyama's much later and more famous use of the same phrase; worth not conflating the two.

The Line Between — this quarrel is restaged in the play, in Kojève's voice.

Freudo-Marxism 1920s–1960s, Reich through the Frankfurt School

ReichCritical TheoryAdorno / HorkheimerMarcuseFromm

Wilhelm Reich was the first to try to fuse Freud and Marx literally, not metaphorically. Through his Sex-Pol clinics in 1920s–30s Vienna and Berlin — free contraception, sex education, adolescent counseling, run as an explicitly political project — he argued the causal arrow ran: capitalism needs a compliant workforce, the patriarchal nuclear family produces that compliance by repressing children's sexuality, and repression's psychic residue is character armor — chronic muscular and psychic rigidity that later makes adults susceptible to authoritarian submission. The Mass Psychology of Fascism (1933) reads Nazism as the political payoff of that repression, not a rational response to economic crisis. The psychoanalytic establishment expelled him in 1934 over the politics; his later turn to "orgone energy" — a claimed measurable life-force he built accumulator boxes to concentrate — cost him the rest of his scientific credibility, and got him prosecuted by the FDA for fraud. He died in a US federal prison in 1957, serving a contempt sentence for defying an injunction against orgone claims.

Between Reich's clinics and Marcuse's New Left fame sits the institution that made Freudo-Marxism a research program rather than a lone theorist's wager. The Frankfurt School — more precisely the Institute for Social Research (Institut für Sozialforschung), founded in Frankfurt in 1923 and directed from 1930 by Max Horkheimer — was a Marxist research institute that, after the Nazi seizure of power, fled via Geneva to Columbia University in New York, reconstituted itself in California exile, and eventually returned to Frankfurt after the war. What Horkheimer named Critical Theory in a 1937 essay was the methodological wager: philosophy and social science should not merely describe social reality but expose the interests that shape it, keeping open an emancipatory possibility that "traditional theory" had closed. The turn to Freud was strategic, not ornamental. Classical Marxism had expected capitalist crisis to produce revolutionary consciousness; fascism, and the failure of large parts of the German working class to resist Nazism, suggested domination was being reproduced inside character structure, family, and mass culture — not only through ownership of the means of production. Freud supplied the vocabulary for that interior: repression, projection, the authoritarian personality. Reich was never Institute staff — Freudo-Marxism has two overlapping but distinct homes — and not everyone inside the Institute agreed how far to go with Freud either.

Theodor W. Adorno and Horkheimer's Dialectic of Enlightenment (written in American exile, 1944; published 1947) is the school's central statement of the darker diagnosis. Enlightenment rationality, tasked with liberating humanity from myth, had itself become a new myth of instrumental domination, paid for in instinctual renunciation; the culture industry — Hollywood, radio, advertising — standardized desire into consumable sameness rather than opening a path out. Their co-authored empirical sequel, The Authoritarian Personality (1950), used psychoanalytic categories (rigid ego-defense, projection, conventionalism) to profile fascism-prone character types in the American population — Critical Theory doing survey psychology, not only speculative dialectics. Walter Benjamin, affiliated rather than core staff, belongs in the same constellation for the aesthetic-political essays that later fed Situationist and cultural-studies readings of commodity spectacle, though he died in 1940 fleeing occupied France, before the California exile phase that produced Dialectic of Enlightenment. The Institute's Freudo-Marxist line, in short, is less "apply Freud to Marx" than: explain why the expected revolutionary subject failed to appear, and treat psychic and cultural reproduction as part of the answer.

Herbert Marcuse, inside that Institute, made the version that traveled furthest politically — and arrived at Freud by way of Hegel, not the other way around: his doctoral and early work was straight Hegel scholarship (Hegel's Ontology and the Theory of Historicity, 1932; Reason and Revolution: Hegel and the Rise of Social Theory, 1941), and the dialectical structure of Eros and Civilization below — repression negated, then negated again into something richer, not simply removed — is a Hegelian argument wearing Freudian vocabulary. Eros and Civilization (1955) splits Freud's repression into basic repression (whatever any workable civilization requires) and surplus-repression (the extra repression a specific social order — capitalism — imposes beyond that, organizing life around what Marcuse calls the performance principle, a capitalist-specific version of the reality principle built around productive labor). Strip the surplus and, in principle, a "non-repressive civilization" organized around play and Eros becomes thinkable — not a return to infantile gratification, but eroticized, non-alienated work and time. One-Dimensional Man (1964) is the pessimistic sequel: advanced capitalism, he argues, learned to neutralize this by repressive desublimation — real sexual liberalization that functions as a release valve and a consumer good rather than a threat, keeping the system's "one-dimensionality" intact. Where Adorno and Horkheimer's late work grows more closed and bleak about emancipation's prospects, Marcuse kept a utopian horizon open — and became the closest thing the New Left had to a house philosopher; 1968 slogans reportedly ran "Marx, Mao, Marcuse."

Erich Fromm, an early Institute member who later drifted from its inner circle, broke further from orthodox Freud than either Adorno or Marcuse — dropping drive theory for a social-character psychology, arguing in Escape from Freedom (1941) that modern freedom itself produces an anxious isolation people resolve by fleeing into authoritarian submission, a milder version of Reich's fascism argument without the sexual-economic mechanics. Fromm and Marcuse fought openly about this in the mid-1950s, in an exchange in Dissent magazine (1955–56): Marcuse accused Fromm's revisionism of quietly adapting psychoanalysis to conformist American culture rather than confronting it. That fight is the Institute's Freudo-Marxist argument in miniature — how much of Freud to keep, how political the remaining theory still is — not a side quarrel between otherwise aligned colleagues.

The French (Post-)Structuralist Turn 1960s–1980s, Althusser to Deleuze & Guattari

ideology & interpellationdurée & the virtualAnti-Oedipusthe repressive hypothesis

Louis Althusser gave the next generation its vocabulary. His essay "Ideology and Ideological State Apparatuses" (1970) argues ideology doesn't reproduce capitalism mainly through force (police, army — the repressive state apparatus) but through ideological state apparatuses — school, church, family, media — which work by interpellation: a policeman shouts "Hey, you there!" and the moment you turn around, you've already recognized yourself as the one being addressed, becoming a subject in exactly the sense the term implies — subjected. Althusser cites Lacan's mirror stage directly as his model: ideology, like the Imaginary, works through a founding misrecognition, not a lie you could simply see through. This is the direct hinge between Marx and Lacan that everything after him has to answer to, including the two students he taught and the movement that reacted against him. Althusser framed his own project explicitly against "Hegelian Marxism" — the humanist reading of the young Marx as continuous with Hegelian dialectics that he saw as sentimental and untheorized — insisting on a properly structural, anti-humanist Marx instead; the irony is that his own machinery for explaining the subject leans on Hegel's dialectic of recognition anyway, just imported through Lacan's Kojève-inflected version of it rather than cited directly.

Behind Deleuze's whole vocabulary sits Henri Bergson (1859–1941) — awarded the 1927 Nobel Prize in Literature, in the committee's words, "in recognition of his rich and vitalizing ideas," for a philosophy written with enough literary force to reach well past the academy. Bergson's durée — lived, qualitative time as a continuous, heterogeneous flow, irreducible to the discrete, spatialized instants clock-time and classical science reduce it to — and his élan vital in Creative Evolution (1907), an unpredictable creative impetus driving life's differentiation against any purely mechanistic account of evolution, gave Deleuze two of his most load-bearing tools: the distinction between the virtual (real but not yet actual, a whole field of potential) and the actual, and a notion of multiplicity that is qualitative and continuous rather than a mere count of discrete things. Deleuze's own book-length engagement, Bergsonism (1966), works this out directly — four years after Nietzsche and Philosophy and six before Anti-Oedipus — closer to a full excavation than a citation, and arguably the deepest philosophical foundation under everything that follows.

Deleuze had already fired the opening shot alone, in Nietzsche and Philosophy (1962), an openly anti-Hegelian book that takes Nietzsche's will to power, above, and reworks it as affirmative difference against what Deleuze reads as Hegelian negation's whole sour economy: desire defined by lack, value defined by opposition, difference only ever mediated through conflict and recognition. That's the direct philosophical root of the "desire as lack, rejected" arrow running from Kojève's Hegel to the pair below, a full decade before he and Guattari made the same argument politically.

Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari answered together with an explosion. Anti-Oedipus: Capitalism and Schizophrenia (1972) attacks Freud, orthodox Lacanianism, and Freudo-Marxism together for the same sin: reducing desire to a private family drama — mommy-daddy-me — when desire is actually a directly social and economic force. Their alternative, desiring-production, treats desire as productive rather than lack-driven (a direct break from both Freud's drive-discharge model and Lacan's desire-as-structural-gap) — a factory, not a wish. Capitalism, on their reading, is itself a desire-machine: it decodes older social codes (kinship, religion, tradition) into free-floating, exploitable flows, then reterritorializes them just enough to keep extracting value, never letting the decoding run all the way. Their proposed alternative to psychoanalysis, schizoanalysis, traces where desire is actually invested in history and society instead of interpreting it back onto Oedipus. Buried in the book is a single, endlessly debated rhetorical question — whether to retreat from capitalism's decoding or go the other way and "accelerate the process" — that later became, more or less by accident, the founding gesture of an entire movement two decades later.

A Thousand Plateaus (1980) supplies that movement's working vocabulary: the rhizome (a horizontal, non-hierarchical network model of thought and organization, against the hierarchical "tree"), the body without organs (a limit-concept for a field of pure intensity prior to being organized into fixed functions, borrowed from Antonin Artaud), and the war machine versus the State apparatus. Every one of these terms resurfaces, mostly unchanged, in the Warwick material below.

Michel Foucault answered from a different angle, and directly against Reich and Marcuse specifically — using a method he credited to Nietzsche by name, above: his essay "Nietzsche, Genealogy, History" (1971) states outright that genealogy is what he's doing to madness, sexuality, and the prison, not a search for hidden essences. The History of Sexuality, Volume 1 (1976) names and rejects the repressive hypothesis — the shared Freudo-Marxist assumption that power has simply repressed sexuality and liberation means removing the repression. Foucault's counter-claim: the modern era didn't silence sex, it produced an enormous, unprecedented proliferation of discourse about it — medical, psychiatric, pedagogical, confessional — deploying sexuality as a technology of biopower, a way of managing populations and bodies that operates by inciting and classifying desire, not suppressing it. If Foucault is right, Reich's entire mechanism — repression in, revolution out — has the relationship backward.

Adjacent to both currents, and worth naming briefly: Jean Baudrillard broke with Marxism outright in The Mirror of Production (1973), arguing its own productivist logic was secretly complicit with capitalism, before moving on to simulacra and hyperreality; Jean-François Lyotard wrote an even more extreme attempt to think economy in openly libidinal terms, Libidinal Economy (1974) — later disowned by Lyotard himself as his "evil book"; and Guy Debord's Situationist Society of the Spectacle (1967) pre-dates and parallels all of it with a diagnosis of domination gone image-based rather than direct.

how this landed

Althusser — interpellation remains a standard tool in ideology critique; Althusser's own biography is complicated by his 1980 killing of his wife during a psychiatric episode, a fact most intellectual histories now note rather than omit.

Warwick / CCRU 1990s–2017, CCRU to Fisher's death

accelerationismhauntologyzombie neoliberalismAcid Communism

The Cybernetic Culture Research Unit was a loosely organized, unofficial research collective inside the University of Warwick's philosophy department through the mid-to-late 1990s — founded around Sadie Plant, joined by Nick Land, and staffed largely by Land's graduate students: Mark Fisher, Robin Mackay, Kodwo Eshun, Iain Hamilton Grant, and others. Its working method mixed dense Deleuze & Guattari exegesis with cyberpunk fiction, jungle and techno music, numerology, and — by most later accounts, including participants' own — genuinely reckless drug experimentation, all folded into a self-consciously "theory-fiction" house style that made truth-claims and horror fiction hard to tell apart on purpose. Much of CCRU's own mythology was subsequently amplified by its former members; treat the group's later reputation as partly self-authored.

Nick Land took Deleuze and Guattari's "accelerate the process" question and answered it without irony — arriving there by way of his own early academic work on Kant and Bataille, including The Thirst for Annihilation (1992). It's also a repurposing of Nietzsche's will to power, above, stripped of Nietzsche's own individualism and rebuilt as something impersonal and machinic — a reading with no real basis in Nietzsche's text, worth flagging given how often his name and Land's get run together. The title Fanged Noumena (2011, his collected CCRU-era writing — "Meltdown" above all) is a direct play on Kant's unreachable thing-in-itself: Land's capital is a literal noumenon, an alien, self-organizing cybernetic intelligence arriving backward through time and using human institutions as a temporary vehicle, dissolving every social code (deterritorialization) with no possible "way out" except further and faster dissolution, not resistance to it — this is accelerationism's actual coinage moment, and it treats the wall Kant put up between phenomena and the thing-in-itself as something capitalism is actively, mechanically breaking through. Land left Warwick and academia by the end of the 1990s amid what most accounts, including Fisher's and Mackay's later reflections, describe as a serious mental health crisis. He resurfaced in Shanghai in the 2000s and, from around 2012, as the author of "The Dark Enlightenment" — a founding text of the online neoreactionary movement, arguing against democracy toward techno-monarchist and explicitly anti-egalitarian politics. It's a hard turn from where the Deleuzoguattarian left assumed this trajectory was heading, and it's one of the most-discussed facts about his career for exactly that reason.

Mark Fisher stayed in the CCRU orbit as a student, then broke from Land's direction entirely. Capitalist Realism: Is There No Alternative? (2009) opens with a line he attributes to Fredric Jameson (and which Slavoj Žižek also used) — that it's easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of capitalism — and diagnoses a specific mood: not active belief that capitalism is good, but the felt impossibility of picturing any coherent alternative, including in the bureaucratic and mental-health fallout of neoliberal work culture, where Fisher argues what looks like a private chemical imbalance is often better read as a political condition. Two of the book's sharper terms for that mood: reflexive impotence, the specifically self-fulfilling despair of young people who already know conditions are dire and have already concluded nothing can change, and a hedonic depression distinct from classical anhedonia — not an inability to feel pleasure, but a subject locked into tightly controlled, commodified loops of pleasure-seeking that never actually discharge into anything. The 2009 book also partly blamed 1960s counterculture's drift into consumerist individualism for helping enable neoliberalism — a reading Fisher later reversed outright, which matters, because Acid Communism, below, is exactly that same counterculture reclaimed rather than indicted. Ghosts of My Life (2014) develops hauntology (a term repurposed from Derrida's play on Marx's specter) into a diagnosis of cultural stagnation — the sense that 20th-century modernism's promised futures got cancelled, leaving culture haunted by lost futures rather than producing new ones.

A 2013 talk at a University of York conference, published as "How to Kill a Zombie: Strategizing the End of Neoliberalism," pushes the diagnosis into strategy. Neoliberalism survived the 2008 crash, Fisher argues, not because anyone was newly convinced by it but because — capitalist realism again — no alternative looked credible enough to replace it with; it persists as a shambling corpse rather than a living conviction. He reads capitalist realism itself as an expression of class decomposition: neoliberalism's real ideological trick was making "competition" look like a natural condition rather than a deliberately engineered fracture of collective solidarity. His proposed correctives cut against reflexive left orthodoxy on two fronts — a critique of anti-hierarchical "horizontalism" as a form fetish that leaves movements easy for organized capital to outmaneuver (an argument Srnicek and Williams later formalize, below, as the case against "folk politics"), and a call for the kind of disciplined, goal-directed strategic thinking the post-1968 left had learned to treat as itself suspect. Underneath both sits a claim that runs through this whole document in one phrasing or another: desire, he says, is always composed, never simply natural, which makes a counter-hegemonic desire something to be actively built rather than discovered — the same wager Reich, Marcuse, and Deleuze and Guattari each made, decades earlier, in their own idiom.

His final, unfinished project — published posthumously from notes as Acid Communism in the k-punk essay collection (2018) — tried to reclaim exactly the Reich/Marcuse-style politicized, consciousness-raising libidinal utopianism of the 1960s–70s counterculture against both capitalist realism's foreclosure of alternatives and Land's nihilist accelerationism: "acid" as both psychedelic and corrosive, a collective rather than individualist repurposing of consciousness-expansion, with Marcuse's Eros and Civilization named directly as a touchstone. His own gloss on the project braided together three strands at once — class consciousness, socialist-feminist consciousness-raising, and psychedelic consciousness — treating the categories people live by as plastic and mutable rather than fixed, with culture itself as the contested site where a post-capitalist subjectivity could actually be cultivated. The same late turn relocated capitalist realism's own origin point: rather than a Cold War, East-versus-West story, his later work traced it to Pinochet's 1973 US-backed coup against Salvador Allende's democratically elected socialist government in Chile — a real, non-Soviet alternative violently ended and rebuilt as neoliberalism's own testing ground, later exported wholesale to Thatcher and Reagan. Fisher died by suicide in January 2017, before completing any of it — a fact his own writing on depression and capitalism makes it impossible to leave out of any honest account of the project.

Briefer but real threads from the same milieu: Sadie Plant's Zeros + Ones (1997) built a cyberfeminist history connecting weaving, Ada Lovelace, and computing before she left Warwick in the late 1990s; Kodwo Eshun's More Brilliant Than the Sun (1998) applied CCRU-style theory-fiction to Afrofuturist sonic culture; and Robin Mackay, through the publisher Urbanomic, co-edited both Fanged Noumena and, with Armen Avanessian, #Accelerate: The Accelerationist Reader (2014) — the anthology that formally canonized "accelerationism" as a named tendency, Land's dark version and the left version below side by side.

Fisher — Capitalist Realism is now a standard reference well beyond academic theory; Acid Communism exists only as an introduction and notes, so its full argument is necessarily reconstructed by readers, not delivered complete.

The Line Between — Fisher's reflexive-impotence reading is restaged in the play.

"How to Kill a Zombie" — talk given at the "Neoliberalism, Crisis and the World System" conference, University of York, July 2013; its critique of horizontalism was contested by parts of the anarchist and autonomist left it was aimed at.

Chile as capitalist realism's origin — a reading of Fisher's later work, not a single named essay with a page number; secondary commentary (e.g. the Acid Horizon podcast's account) traces the argument across scattered late writing and talks rather than one canonical text.

Other connected strains 2009–present

left-accelerationismxenofeminismsemiocapitalism

Nick Srnicek and Alex Williams's "#Accelerate: Manifesto for an Accelerationist Politics" (2013) is the explicit attempt to take acceleration back from Land: rather than rejecting modernity and technology (their target is as much what they call "folk politics" — the nostalgic, localist, anti-strategic left already targeted in Fisher's own zombie-neoliberalism talk, above — as it is capitalism itself), the left should seize and redirect the technical and infrastructural capacities capitalism has already built. Inventing the Future (2015) turns this into a concrete program — automation, full employment's abolition rather than its defense, universal basic income — a fairly direct descendant of the Fisher-era wish to reclaim a future capitalist realism had foreclosed.

The collective Laboria Cuboniks's "Xenofeminism: A Politics for Alienation" (2015) pushes the same accelerationist-adjacent, tech-affirmative stance through an explicitly feminist lens — embracing artificiality and alienation from any given "nature" rather than defending it, a lineage that runs back through Plant's cyberfeminism as much as through Land or Srnicek and Williams.

Franco "Bifo" Berardi arrives from a different national tradition entirely — Italian autonomism/operaismo, not French post-structuralism or Warwick — but converges on close to the same diagnosis. The Soul at Work and Heroes: Mass Murder and Suicide theorize semiocapitalism: a capitalism that has moved from exploiting labor-time to exploiting attention and nervous systems directly, producing burnout, panic, and depression as its characteristic pathologies. Berardi and Fisher were friends and interlocutors — Fisher leans directly on Berardi's own reading of the failed 2010–11 uprisings in the "How to Kill a Zombie" talk, above — and the kinship here is real dialogue between parallel traditions, not one descending from the other.

Jodi Dean's communicative capitalism (from essays collected across Democracy and Other Neoliberal Fantasies, 2009, and Blog Theory, 2010) makes a related but distinct claim: digital communication technology doesn't enable the democratic participation it promises, it captures political energy as circulating content — messages, shares, drive without discharge — which functions to neutralize rather than build collective action. Dean writes in explicit dialogue with both Žižek's drive theory and the post-Fisher accelerationist debates.

how this landed

Left-accelerationism — remains a live, debated position on the Marxist left; critics on the same side argue it underestimates ecological limits and overestimates technology's neutrality.

Xenofeminism — influential in academic feminist theory and art/design circles; less discussed in mainstream political theory than Srnicek and Williams's version.

Berardi / Dean — both are treated as serious, independent contemporary critical theorists, not derivative of the Warwick lineage, despite the thematic overlap.

Visual mapping

The line starts further back than Freud or Marx: Kant splits the subject from the thing-in-itself and the "I think" from any soul-substance; Hegel dialecticizes that split into a struggle for recognition; Kojève reads that struggle as a theory of desire, which Freudo-Marxism and the Frankfurt School sit beside rather than descend from, and which Deleuze & Guattari later reject outright. From there — Freudo-Marxism gets rejected by French theory for reducing desire to family drama; French theory's own Deleuze & Guattari get radicalized at Warwick into accelerationism; Warwick then splits into a communist return to Marcuse (Fisher) and a neoreactionary exit (Land) — Land's own version, fittingly, put back in explicitly Kantian terms. Solid lines build on; dashed lines critique or break from; the thick cyan line is the single explicit callback across the whole genealogy.

The neuroscience piece this branches from was asking one question about Freud: is any of this actually in the brain. This branch asks a completely different one: whose interests does the unconscious serve, and can it be organized — toward liberation, as Reich and Marcuse and Fisher each tried in their own idiom, or toward whatever Land thinks he's accelerating toward. Neither question settles the other. A construct like the id can be a reasonably well-evidenced claim about the SEEKING system (see the neuroscience map) and, completely independently, a live political stake in an argument that's been running for a century and hasn't stopped.

What's striking in hindsight is how much of the running argument is the same argument: Kant says the subject is a function, not a substance, Hegel says that function only completes itself through another's recognition, Kojève says that's a theory of desire, Deleuze and Guattari say no, desire is production, not lack, Reich says repression produces fascism, Foucault says power doesn't repress it produces, Land says accelerate anyway — in Kant's own vocabulary, chasing the noumenon — and Fisher says no, go back to Marcuse and mean it this time. Every generation in this lineage thought it was correcting the one before. Read end to end, it looks more like the same unresolved argument about desire and recognition that started in Königsberg and Jena, worked over by people who kept disagreeing about which direction "liberation" points.

Glossary

The key terms from each current, grouped the way the sections above are — a place to look one up without re-reading the paragraph it came from.

Kant & Hegel

Phenomena / noumena
Kant's split between things as a structuring mind necessarily experiences them (phenomena) and things as they are in themselves, permanently unreachable (noumena).
Transcendental unity of apperception
Kant's "I think" that must be able to accompany all of a subject's representations — a formal precondition for unified experience, not a persisting soul-substance.
Dialectics / dialectical
A way of thinking in which concepts, social forms, or stages of consciousness develop by generating their own contradictions and being transformed through them — not a static either/or. In Hegel (and downstream in Marx, the Frankfurt School, and Marcuse), a relation is dialectical when each side only becomes what it is through the other, and the conflict between them produces a new shape rather than a simple winner.
Lordship and bondage
Hegel's master-slave dialectic: self-consciousness achieves itself only through a struggle for recognition with another self-consciousness, producing an unequal relation that then dialectically inverts.

Nietzsche

Genealogy (method)
Nietzsche's method of explaining a value or category by its contingent history of descent — accidents, reversals, power struggles — rather than by an essence or a resolving dialectic.
Master / slave morality
Nietzsche's own master/slave story, distinct from Hegel's lordship and bondage above: master morality calls the strong's own vitality "good"; slave morality inverts the scale, declaring that vitality evil and the powerless's patience "good" instead.
Ressentiment
Nietzsche's term for the reactive, self-poisoning resentment of the powerless, unable to discharge itself in action and discharging in moral judgment instead.
Bad conscience
Nietzsche's account of guilt and conscience as outward cruelty and aggression, walled in by society and turned back against their own owner — the direct textual ancestor of Freud's superego.
Will to power
Nietzsche's proposal that drives, institutions, and acts of knowing are best read as forms of power seeking to expand and overcome resistance, not neutral facts. Badly distorted by his sister's posthumous editing into something aligned with politics Nietzsche rejected; read Deleuze's and Land's very different later uses of the term against that caveat.

Kojève's Hegel

Desire for recognition
Kojève's reading of Hegelian desire: not desire for objects, but desire to be desired and acknowledged by another desiring consciousness.
End of history
Kojève's claim that the historical dialectic of recognition had, on his reading, effectively resolved itself — not to be confused with Francis Fukuyama's later, unrelated use of the same phrase.

Freudo-Marxism / Frankfurt School

Frankfurt School
Shorthand for the Institute for Social Research (founded Frankfurt 1923; Horkheimer director from 1930) and the circle around it — Critical Theory's institutional home, forced into American exile under Nazism, later returned to Frankfurt.
Critical Theory
Horkheimer's name (1937) for a philosophy and social science that doesn't merely describe social reality but exposes the interests shaping it, keeping emancipatory possibility open against "traditional theory."
Culture industry
Adorno and Horkheimer's term for mass culture (film, radio, advertising) as a system that standardizes desire into consumable sameness, integrating subjects into domination rather than offering an exit from it.
Character armor
Reich's term for chronic muscular and psychic rigidity built up to defend against anxiety, later making a person susceptible to authoritarian submission.
Pleasure principle
Freud's name for the id's governing tendency: seek immediate discharge of tension and avoid unpleasure, without regard for external constraints, contradiction, or time.
Reality principle
The ego's governing tendency, learned against the pleasure principle: postpone or reshape gratification according to what the external world will actually allow — the psychic cost of civilization as Freud saw it.
Eros
Freud's life drive (also called the sexual or libidinal drive in a broad sense): the binding, unifying force that builds larger unities — bodies, bonds, culture — against dissolution. Marcuse's Eros and Civilization treats a liberated Eros as the possible basis of a non-repressive social order.
Death drive (Thanatos)
Freud's late counterpart to Eros (introduced in Beyond the Pleasure Principle, 1920): a drive toward tension-reduction at its limit — inorganic stillness, repetition, aggression turned outward or against the self. "Thanatos" is later scholarly shorthand, not Freud's own label.
Basic repression / surplus-repression
Marcuse's split between the repression any workable civilization requires (basic) and the extra repression a specific social order imposes beyond that (surplus).
Performance principle
Marcuse's term for capitalism's specific organization of the reality principle around productive labor.
Repressive desublimation
Marcuse's term for liberalized sexuality that functions as a release valve and consumer good rather than a genuine threat to the system.

French (Post-)Structuralism

Structuralism
The mid-century approach, descending from Saussure's linguistics via Lévi-Strauss, Lacan, and Althusser, that treats culture, kinship, the psyche, and ideology as systems of relations — differences with no positive terms — rather than as expressions of a prior, self-present subject or meaning.
Post-structuralism
The cluster of responses (Foucault, Derrida, Deleuze & Guattari, and others) that keep structuralism's anti-humanism and relational method, but refuse the idea of a closed, stable system: structures are historically contingent, riven by power, desire, and play, and never finally determine what they organize.
Durée
Bergson's term for lived, qualitative time — a continuous, heterogeneous flow, irreducible to the discrete instants clock-time reduces it to.
Élan vital
Bergson's "vital impetus": an unpredictable, creative force driving life's differentiation, against any purely mechanistic account of evolution.
Virtual / actual
Bergson's distinction, inherited directly by Deleuze: the virtual is real but not yet actualized, a whole field of potential rather than a merely possible thing.
Interpellation
Althusser's term for how ideology constitutes individuals as subjects by "hailing" them — the moment of recognizing oneself as the one being addressed.
Ideological state apparatuses
Althusser's term for the diffuse institutions — school, church, family, media — that reproduce capitalist relations through ideology rather than force.
Desiring-production
Deleuze and Guattari's model of desire as inherently productive, not structured by lack — a direct rejection of both the Freudian and Lacanian pictures.
Deterritorialization / reterritorialization
D&G's term for capitalism dissolving old social codes (deterritorialization) while reimposing just enough new coding to keep extracting value (reterritorialization).
Schizoanalysis
D&G's proposed alternative to psychoanalysis: tracing where desire is actually invested in history and society, instead of interpreting it back onto the Oedipal family.
Repressive hypothesis
The assumption, which Foucault rejects, that power has simply repressed sexuality and that liberation just means removing the repression.
Biopower
Foucault's term for power exercised by managing populations and bodies through incitement and classification, not only prohibition.

Warwick / CCRU

Accelerationism
The position, following a disputed line in Anti-Oedipus, that capitalism's dissolving of social codes should be pushed further and faster rather than resisted.
Theory-fiction
CCRU's house style, blending philosophical argument with speculative and horror fiction until the two become hard to tell apart on purpose.
Hauntology
Fisher's term (via Derrida) for a culture haunted by futures that were promised but never arrived.
Reflexive impotence
Fisher's term for a self-fulfilling despair: knowing conditions are dire while already having concluded nothing can change.
Zombie neoliberalism
Fisher's term for neoliberalism after 2008: discredited as a conviction, but persisting as a shambling system with no credible alternative yet in place.
Class decomposition
Fisher's reading of capitalist realism as neoliberalism's deliberate fracturing of collective working-class solidarity, repackaged as natural "competition."
Hedonic depression
Fisher's term for a depression distinct from classical anhedonia — not an inability to feel pleasure, but pleasure-seeking locked into tightly controlled, commodified loops.
Neoreaction / Dark Enlightenment
The anti-democratic, techno-monarchist politics Land moved toward after leaving Warwick — a rupture from, not a continuation of, his CCRU-era work.

Contemporary

Folk politics
Srnicek and Williams's term for horizontalist, localist, anti-strategic left tactics they argue are outmatched by organized capital — echoing Fisher's own critique.
Semiocapitalism
Bifo Berardi's term for a capitalism that exploits attention and nervous systems directly, rather than labor-time.
Communicative capitalism
Jodi Dean's term for digital communication technology capturing political energy as circulating content rather than enabling democratic action.
Xenofeminism
Laboria Cuboniks's tech-affirmative, artificiality-embracing feminist politics — descended as much from Plant's cyberfeminism as from accelerationism proper.
